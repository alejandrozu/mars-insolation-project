# Mars Insolation Project

An interactive simulator of daylight and sunlight on Mars across its eccentric year.

**Live site:** https://alejandrozu.github.io/mars-insolation-project/

**Guide:** https://alejandrozu.github.io/mars-insolation-project/guide.html, in four parts: every choice behind the default three-sun world and why it was made (the goal, the mirrors at L4 and L5, UV-free light, the air, the ocean and lakes, the lapse rate, the five seasons and the calendar); how the model works in detail (orbit, sunlight, mirror shaping, UV, the energy balance, day and night, winds and rain, the Earth tests, lakes, rivers, the season fit, the map); the quirks of Mars; and how to use the simulator, with a glossary. The simulator links to it from its header.

Mars has a sol close to an Earth day and an axial tilt close to Earth's (25.19°), but its orbit is far more eccentric (e = 0.0934). The seasons have unequal lengths (154 / 193 / 179 / 143 sols) and sunlight at perihelion is about 45% stronger than at aphelion (713 vs 490 W/m²), so the length and strength of daylight behave very differently from Earth.

## What it does

- **Mars view**: the orbit drawn to scale, with season wedges whose areas follow Kepler's second law, plus a side view of the tilt. A ground view shows the sky dome at any latitude, with sun paths in local true solar time.
- **Date dial and calendar**: a circular control for the whole year, starting at New Year (Ls 0°, the northern spring equinox), with an outer ring for the twenty months of the three-sun calendar and an inner ring for its five seasons (Autumn, Winter, Spring, Summer and Estia, after the five traditional Spanish seasons; Estia is the late, dry summer), squares at the solstices and equinoxes and dots at perihelion and aphelion. Each season has four months of 6-sol weeks (36-sol months of six weeks, 30-sol months of five): Autumn, Winter and Spring last 138 sols, Summer and Estia 127, and two festival sols sit outside the weeks (Perihelia, the 31st of Calidor, and Finalia, the 31st of Finival, with Finalia II in leap years). The dial can show the date as month and day, as the sol of the year and as Ls, in any combination. A play button runs through the year in anything from 1 to 300 seconds.
- **Mars map**: MOLA topography in equirectangular projection, with its controls above the 2:1 map. Water: an adjustable water level (share of the surface below sea level, 26% by default, which puts 23.8% of Mars under the ocean joined to the northern lowlands), Hellas and Argyre as closed seas with their own surfaces or held at sea level, and every closed depression holding a lake: by default the lake its own water balance sustains (each rises until evaporation from its surface balances the runoff and overflow coming in, and spills downstream when full; with the default sky Hellas and Argyre fill to their brims and spill, the wet end of what is possible, and the lakes are re-balanced when the sky changes), or one fill everywhere with Hellas and Argyre set by hand; at the defaults that is a global layer of about 475 m of water covering 45% of the planet. Heights in km or Earth-equivalent km (× 0.38, the same drop in air pressure), three colour schemes, and layers over land: insolation in W/m² (any date or the yearly average, with 100 W/m² lines); an insolation-based temperature in °C, °F or K (any date, the yearly average, the seasonal swing, or each place's 10th warmest or 10th coldest sol; each as the daily average, the daily high or the daily low, from a day–night model fitted to Earth's daily temperature ranges and scaled for the longer sol, the suns' timing and the 2.77× thicker air column), from a seasonal energy-balance model calibrated on Earth's climate, with continental, mixed or maritime settings or a dynamic setting (the default) on a latitude–longitude grid with the map's own ocean, closed seas and lakes, an adjustable sea-level air pressure (1.05 atm by default), the air's CO₂ (1,000 ppm by default; under Mars's deep air column that holds as much CO₂ overhead as about 2,770 ppm on Earth, and adds 5.35 ln(column × ppm / 350) W/m² to the Earth-fitted greenhouse) and cooling with height at an adjustable lapse rate (6 K per Earth-equivalent km by default, the middle of what mountain stations on Earth measure for daily mean temperatures; Earth's standard atmosphere uses 6.5 for the free air), with isotherms every 1–10 °C in half-degree steps (or 1–20 °F) counted from freezing and a colour scale that spends most of its range on 0–35 °C (dark capri just above freezing, green at 10 °C, yellow 20, orange 30, pure red 35, then darker, ashy reds; a light azure below 0 °C that turns bluer and darker with the cold); and rain, yearly or on the chosen date, from a moisture-budget model on a 2° grid driven by those temperatures. The model includes evaporation from the ocean, lakes and land (with a Budyko soil-water balance), Hadley, Ferrel and monsoon winds from the temperature contrasts (the air rises wherever near-surface moist static energy peaks, even near a pole), rising and sinking air, storm tracks, terrain that holds moist low air back, and lift over slopes. Its constants were fitted on Earth's observed rain and closed-basin lakes. Run on Earth the way it runs here, its rain over land correlates 0.63 with the observed, typically within a factor of about 2; its summer monsoons are too weak and its closed basins too full. Rain is shown on a doubling colour scale (300 mm a year yellow, 600 mm green, 1.2 m cyan, 2.4 m purple, 4.8 m magenta) with rain lines every 10 mm to 1 m a year, the gap doubling past 600 mm and again past 2.4 m (the 300 mm line thick). The rain and temperature layers can colour the ocean, closed seas and lakes too, with their coasts outlined. Height and depth lines can be switched on and drawn every 0.1–5 km, every set of lines and the coastlines can be drawn thinner or thicker, the layer's own lines can be switched off, any set of lines can fill its bands with solid colours, a land-and-water colour scheme shows just where the water is, and the readout gives the water the map holds as a global equivalent layer (475 m at the three-sun defaults, 274 m with two suns). Climate zones: each place's Köppen–Geiger class, or Martian zones drawn up for this world (H hot, W warm, M mild, K cool, C cold, F frigid or P polar by the year's mean temperature; rainy, humid, dry or desert from rain over the rain model's potential evaporation; even or uneven rain for the wetter warm places; a leading s for seasonal climates), from twelve months of temperature and rain. Clicking zones in the legend highlights them on the map. Rivers can be drawn on any layer: the rain model's runoff routed downhill and through the basins that spill, exaggerated in width (on by default; flows above about 25 m³/s), carrying on across the lakes they pass through so a chain of lakes reads as one river. The rain for the default skies is stored in the page, so it and the rivers show at once; other skies take a few seconds to compute. Latitude and longitude lines every 1–30°, landing sites, zoom and pan to any region (the flat map wraps round east–west), and a 3D globe view (WebGL) that wraps the same map on a sphere with the relief exaggerated (×10 by default, adjustable), to turn and zoom by hand. The map's controls can sit above it or in a column on its left that can be dragged wider or narrower, and the date (as the dial shows it) and its season can be shown in the map's corner.
- **Light by wavelength**: every insolation view (the insolation and ratio charts, the map's insolation layer, the readouts) can show all the radiation, visible light only (400–700 nm, 39% of sunlight) or the clear-sky UV index at the ground (Madronich's formula with an Earth-like ozone layer, the Sun's distance and the air column overhead; under Mars gravity 1.05 atm is 2.77× Earth's air). The mirrors are UV-free by default: they reflect nothing below 400 nm and correspondingly more visible and infrared light, so each still delivers its stated total and only the Sun sends ultraviolet. The tropical noon UV index at sea level is then about 2.5–3.5, against 10–12 on Earth. The swap warms the climate like about 2% more mirror light, because the mirrors' UV would have heated the stratosphere instead of the lower air: about 1.3 °C at the defaults, with 9% more rain with three suns (a switch turns it off for comparison).
- **Reset buttons**: each screen and the sky settings have a Reset button that puts that screen's settings back to the defaults.
- **Temperature by latitude**: the insolation-based temperature over each latitude's land (no ocean, no lakes, no ground above a cut-off of 3 km in Earth-equivalent height by default), as a yearly average with the coldest-to-warmest band, plus the seasonal swing, with an optional comparison to the same sky without counterirradiance. The readout counts the sols a year below 0 °C and above 30 °C, land-weighted, with each patch of land cooled with its own height. It also gives the ocean north of the equator (yearly average and sols between 20 and 30 °C) and land poleward of 45°, south and north (average swing and sols below −20 °C or above 30 °C).
- **Daylight duration**, **insolation** (daily mean or peak) and **seasonal ratio** (brightest ÷ dimmest, log scale) across the year for any set of latitudes, or a whole spectrum of them.
- **Circular orbit**: each chart can show the real orbit, the real orbit with the e = 0 planet as a dashed reference, or the circular planet alone.
- **Align southern seasons**: plots southern latitudes from their own winter solstice.
- **Two and three suns** (three suns by default): mirror arrays at the Sun–Mars L4 and/or L5 points, at strengths that can bring the average flux to Earth's level. With two suns, a counterirradiance setting (k from 0 to 1) shapes the mirror light like a concave lens, 1 − k·cos 2a across the disc it lights (a from the disc's east–west centre line), dimmer in the middle and brighter toward the north and south edges, with the average kept the same; a phase setting moves the dimmest line north or south, fixed or following the seasons (default: mirrors at L5, k = 1 swinging by ±0.2, the dimmest line swinging ±27.5° with the seasons plus a ±6.5° semiannual part around a line 11.5° north of the centre: chosen with the map's defaults for the most rain and the fewest deserts while keeping land between 45°S and 45°N inside 0–30 °C on all but about 24 sols a year with a 30% sea and 5 K/km; about 33 with UV-free mirrors, the default 26% sea and 6 K/km). Three suns sets the strength and shaping of each array separately: L5, the second sun, keeps the lens profile and moderates the middle latitudes, while L4, the supplemental sun, can light only the outer latitudes, beyond a north edge and a south edge that never come inside ±45° (by default the north edge stays at 50°N or beyond), with a soft edge, and with its edges, north/south split and strength following the seasons (set at Ls 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°, joined smoothly), so the populated band sees only two suns and keeps its nights. Its default (L5 1.10× Sun; L4 up to 0.51×, 0.32× on average), chosen with the map's wet-climate defaults and the earlier rain model, and nudged toward more rain on the southern highlands; its north edge was later moved from 45–52°N to 50–57°N, with more of its light sent north so the northern cap gets the same light, which cools the populated band a little. Last, with the lapse rate raised to 6 K/km, L4 sends less of its northern-summer light north and L5's seasonal swing was retuned, which takes the band's sols above 30 °C from about 12 to 2 a year without adding frost or drying the land. With the CO₂ setting at its default 1,000 ppm, L5 went from 1.17× to 1.10×, which leaves the planet a little warmer and wetter. With the lakes the current rain model gives and UV-free mirrors, land between 45°S and 45°N is outside 0–30 °C on about 8 sols a year at the default 26% sea, 6 K/km and 1,000 ppm of CO₂, 2 of them frost on the highest ground; the ocean north of the equator stays between 20 and 30 °C all year (24–27 °C), land poleward of 45°S is outside −20 to 30 °C on fewer than 1 sol, and land poleward of 45°N passes 30 °C on about 43. The shift, bias, semiannual and k-swing controls sit in a Fine-tune box that starts closed; they apply either way. When it follows the seasons, a shift moves the swing along the year and a bias offsets it north or south, a semiannual term lets the two halves of the year differ, and k itself can swing over the year: p = bias + A × sin(Ls − shift) + A₂ × sin 2(Ls − s₂), k(Ls) = k + a × sin(Ls − s). The insolation-based temperature defaults to the dynamic setting (the map’s own land and water) at 1.05 atm.
- **Earth comparison** lines, Mars or Earth hours, dynamic or fixed axes, and full-screen or compact layouts.

## Model

Keplerian orbit (a = 1.5237 AU, e = 0.0934, perihelion at Ls 250.9°), year of 686.98 days (668.6 sols), top-of-atmosphere insolation from 1361 W/m² at 1 AU, and sunrise when the Sun's centre crosses a flat horizon. Today's Mars date uses the Allison & McEwen (2000) formula. The insolation-based temperature comes from a two-box (land and sea) seasonal energy-balance model per 2° latitude band, after North & Coakley (1979): Earth's outgoing-radiation fit (203.3 + 2.09·T W/m²), Earth's cloud albedo without snow or sea ice, diffusive poleward heat transport (0.649 W/m²/K), and heat capacities and land–sea exchange fitted to Earth's seasonal cycle. On Earth's orbit it gives a 15 °C global mean and −7 / 23 °C (January / July) on land at 45°N. Elevation comes from the Mars Global Surveyor MOLA gridded topography (MEGDR, 4 pixels per degree; Smith et al. 2003, NASA PDS Geosciences Node), packed into `mola-4ppd.txt` (5 m steps, row-delta coded, zlib-compressed, base64). Everything runs in the browser from `index.html` and that one data file, with no build step and no server.
