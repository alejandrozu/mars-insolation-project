# Mars Insolation Project

An interactive simulator of daylight and sunlight on Mars across its eccentric year.

**Live site:** https://alejandrozu.github.io/mars-insolation-project/

Mars has a sol close to an Earth day and an axial tilt close to Earth's (25.19°), but its orbit is far more eccentric (e = 0.0934). The seasons have unequal lengths (154 / 193 / 179 / 143 sols) and sunlight at perihelion is about 45% stronger than at aphelion (713 vs 490 W/m²), so the length and strength of daylight behave very differently from Earth.

## What it does

- **Mars view**: the orbit drawn to scale, with season wedges whose areas follow Kepler's second law, plus a side view of the tilt. A ground view shows the sky dome at any latitude, with sun paths in local true solar time.
- **Date dial**: a circular control for the whole year, starting at the northern winter solstice, with the four northern seasons drawn to their real lengths (154 / 193 / 179 / 143 sols) and perihelion and aphelion marked.
- **Mars map**: MOLA topography in equirectangular projection with an adjustable water level (share of the surface below sea level, 30% by default), an option to fill every crater and closed basin with a lake (from dry to full to the brim), heights in km or Earth-equivalent km (× 0.38, the same drop in air pressure), three colour schemes, an insolation layer for land (any date, the yearly average, or for temperature the seasonal swing, warmest minus coldest sol) shown in W/m² with 100 W/m² iso-insolation lines, or as an insolation-based temperature in °C, °F or K (from a seasonal energy-balance model calibrated on Earth's climate, with continental, mixed or maritime settings or a dynamic setting (the default) that runs the model on a latitude–longitude grid with the map's own oceans, Hellas, Argyre and lakes and Earth-like prevailing winds, an adjustable sea-level air pressure, 1 atm by default, that sets how much heat the land and air hold and how easily it moves, and cooling with height at an adjustable lapse rate) with isotherms counted from 0 °C at an adjustable spacing (every 10 °C by default), blended over the elevation, latitude and longitude lines every 1–30°, landing sites, and zoom and pan to any region.
- **Reset buttons**: each screen and the sky settings have a Reset button that puts that screen's settings back to the defaults.
- **Temperature by latitude**: the insolation-based temperature over each latitude's land (no ocean, no lakes, no ground above a cut-off of 3 km in Earth-equivalent height by default), as a yearly average with the coldest-to-warmest band, plus the seasonal swing, with an optional comparison to the same sky without counterirradiance. The readout counts the sols a year below 0 °C and above 30 °C, land-weighted, with each patch of land cooled with its own height.
- **Daylight duration**, **insolation** (daily mean or peak) and **seasonal ratio** (brightest ÷ dimmest, log scale) across the year for any set of latitudes, or a whole spectrum of them.
- **Circular orbit**: each chart can show the real orbit, the real orbit with the e = 0 planet as a dashed reference, or the circular planet alone.
- **Align southern seasons**: plots southern latitudes from their own winter solstice.
- **Two and three suns**: mirror arrays at the Sun–Mars L4 and/or L5 points, at strengths that can bring the average flux to Earth's level. With two suns, a counterirradiance setting (k from 0 to 1) shapes the mirror light like a concave lens, 1 − k·cos 2a across the disc it lights (a from the disc's east–west centre line), dimmer in the middle and brighter toward the north and south edges, with the average kept the same; a phase setting moves the dimmest line north or south, fixed or following the seasons (default: mirrors at L5, k = 1 swinging down to 0.4 around Ls 171°, the dimmest line swinging ±27.5° with the seasons plus a ±6.5° semiannual part around a line 11.5° north of the centre: the setting that gives the fewest sols below 0 °C or above 30 °C on land between 45°S and 45°N at 1 atm, in both the dynamic and continental settings). When it follows the seasons, a shift moves the swing along the year and a bias offsets it north or south, a semiannual term lets the two halves of the year differ, and k itself can swing over the year: p = bias + A × sin(Ls − shift) + A₂ × sin 2(Ls − s₂), k(Ls) = k + a × sin(Ls − s). The insolation-based temperature defaults to the dynamic setting (the map’s own land and water) at 1 atm.
- **Earth comparison** lines, Mars or Earth hours, dynamic or fixed axes, and full-screen or compact layouts.

## Model

Keplerian orbit (a = 1.5237 AU, e = 0.0934, perihelion at Ls 250.9°), year of 686.98 days (668.6 sols), top-of-atmosphere insolation from 1361 W/m² at 1 AU, and sunrise when the Sun's centre crosses a flat horizon. Today's Mars date uses the Allison & McEwen (2000) formula. The insolation-based temperature comes from a two-box (land and sea) seasonal energy-balance model per 2° latitude band, after North & Coakley (1979): Earth's outgoing-radiation fit (203.3 + 2.09·T W/m²), Earth's cloud albedo without snow or sea ice, diffusive poleward heat transport (0.649 W/m²/K), and heat capacities and land–sea exchange fitted to Earth's seasonal cycle. On Earth's orbit it gives a 15 °C global mean and −7 / 23 °C (January / July) on land at 45°N. Elevation comes from the Mars Global Surveyor MOLA gridded topography (MEGDR, 4 pixels per degree; Smith et al. 2003, NASA PDS Geosciences Node), packed into `mola-4ppd.txt` (5 m steps, row-delta coded, zlib-compressed, base64). Everything runs in the browser from `index.html` and that one data file, with no build step and no server.
