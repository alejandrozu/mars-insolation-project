# Mars Insolation Project

An interactive simulator of daylight and sunlight on Mars across its eccentric year.

**Live site:** https://alejandrozu.github.io/mars-insolation-project/

Mars has a sol close to an Earth day and an axial tilt close to Earth's (25.19°), but its orbit is far more eccentric (e = 0.0934). The seasons have unequal lengths (154 / 193 / 179 / 143 sols) and sunlight at perihelion is about 45% stronger than at aphelion (713 vs 490 W/m²), so the length and strength of daylight behave very differently from Earth.

## What it does

- **Mars view**: the orbit drawn to scale, with season wedges whose areas follow Kepler's second law, plus a side view of the tilt. A ground view shows the sky dome at any latitude, with sun paths in local true solar time.
- **Date dial**: a circular control for the whole year, starting at the northern winter solstice, with the four northern seasons drawn to their real lengths (154 / 193 / 179 / 143 sols) and perihelion and aphelion marked.
- **Mars map**: MOLA topography in equirectangular projection, with its controls above the 2:1 map. Water: an adjustable water level (share of the surface below sea level, 30% by default, which puts 27.4% of Mars under the ocean joined to the northern lowlands), Hellas and Argyre as closed seas with their own surfaces or held at sea level, and every closed depression holding a lake: by default the lake its own water balance sustains (each rises until evaporation from its surface balances the runoff and overflow coming in, and spills downstream when full; Hellas settles about 3.5 km below sea level, Argyre about 0.5 km below, and the lakes are re-balanced when the sky changes), or one fill everywhere with Hellas and Argyre set by hand; at the defaults that is a global layer of about 374 m of water covering 37.2% of the planet. Heights in km or Earth-equivalent km (× 0.38, the same drop in air pressure), three colour schemes, and layers over land: insolation in W/m² (any date or the yearly average, with 100 W/m² lines); an insolation-based temperature in °C, °F or K (any date, the yearly average, the seasonal swing, or each place's 10th warmest or 10th coldest sol), from a seasonal energy-balance model calibrated on Earth's climate, with continental, mixed or maritime settings or a dynamic setting (the default) on a latitude–longitude grid with the map's own ocean, closed seas and lakes, an adjustable sea-level air pressure (1.05 atm by default) and cooling with height at an adjustable lapse rate (5 K per Earth-equivalent km by default, the moist lapse rate of a rainy climate), with isotherms counted from 0 °C; and rain, yearly or on the chosen date, from a moisture-budget model on a 2° grid driven by those temperatures (evaporation from the ocean, lakes and land, winds from the temperature contrasts, rain-out and lift over slopes). Rain is shown on a doubling colour scale (300 mm a year yellow, 600 mm green, 1.2 m cyan, 2.4 m purple, 4.8 m magenta), and the temperature layer can colour the ocean, closed seas and lakes by their surface temperature too. Rivers can be drawn on any layer: the rain model's runoff routed downhill and through the basins that spill, exaggerated in width. Latitude and longitude lines every 1–30°, landing sites, zoom and pan to any region, and a 3D globe view (WebGL) that wraps the same map on a sphere with the relief exaggerated (×10 by default, adjustable), to turn and zoom by hand.
- **Reset buttons**: each screen and the sky settings have a Reset button that puts that screen's settings back to the defaults.
- **Temperature by latitude**: the insolation-based temperature over each latitude's land (no ocean, no lakes, no ground above a cut-off of 3 km in Earth-equivalent height by default), as a yearly average with the coldest-to-warmest band, plus the seasonal swing, with an optional comparison to the same sky without counterirradiance. The readout counts the sols a year below 0 °C and above 30 °C, land-weighted, with each patch of land cooled with its own height. It also gives the ocean north of the equator (yearly average and sols between 20 and 30 °C) and land poleward of 45°, south and north (average swing and sols below −20 °C or above 30 °C).
- **Daylight duration**, **insolation** (daily mean or peak) and **seasonal ratio** (brightest ÷ dimmest, log scale) across the year for any set of latitudes, or a whole spectrum of them.
- **Circular orbit**: each chart can show the real orbit, the real orbit with the e = 0 planet as a dashed reference, or the circular planet alone.
- **Align southern seasons**: plots southern latitudes from their own winter solstice.
- **Two and three suns**: mirror arrays at the Sun–Mars L4 and/or L5 points, at strengths that can bring the average flux to Earth's level. With two suns, a counterirradiance setting (k from 0 to 1) shapes the mirror light like a concave lens, 1 − k·cos 2a across the disc it lights (a from the disc's east–west centre line), dimmer in the middle and brighter toward the north and south edges, with the average kept the same; a phase setting moves the dimmest line north or south, fixed or following the seasons (default: mirrors at L5, k = 1 swinging by ±0.6, the dimmest line swinging ±27.5° with the seasons plus a ±6.5° semiannual part around a line 11.5° north of the centre: the fewest sols below 0 °C or above 30 °C on land between 45°S and 45°N at 1 atm). Three suns sets the strength and shaping of each array separately: L5, the second sun, keeps the lens profile and moderates the middle latitudes, while L4, the supplemental sun, can light only the outer latitudes, beyond north and south edges that never come inside ±45°, with a soft edge, and with its edges, north/south split and strength following the seasons (set at Ls 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°, joined smoothly), so the populated band sees only two suns and keeps its nights. Its default (L5 1.17× Sun; L4 up to 0.51×, 0.32× on average), chosen with the map's wet-climate defaults and nudged toward more rain on the southern highlands, keeps land between 45°S and 45°N inside 0–30 °C on all but about 7 sols a year, the ocean north of the equator between 20 and 30 °C all year (21–24 °C), land south of 45°S inside −20 to 30 °C on all but about 1 sol and land north of 45°N on every sol. The shift, bias, semiannual and k-swing controls sit in a Fine-tune box that starts closed; they apply either way. When it follows the seasons, a shift moves the swing along the year and a bias offsets it north or south, a semiannual term lets the two halves of the year differ, and k itself can swing over the year: p = bias + A × sin(Ls − shift) + A₂ × sin 2(Ls − s₂), k(Ls) = k + a × sin(Ls − s). The insolation-based temperature defaults to the dynamic setting (the map’s own land and water) at 1.05 atm.
- **Earth comparison** lines, Mars or Earth hours, dynamic or fixed axes, and full-screen or compact layouts.

## Model

Keplerian orbit (a = 1.5237 AU, e = 0.0934, perihelion at Ls 250.9°), year of 686.98 days (668.6 sols), top-of-atmosphere insolation from 1361 W/m² at 1 AU, and sunrise when the Sun's centre crosses a flat horizon. Today's Mars date uses the Allison & McEwen (2000) formula. The insolation-based temperature comes from a two-box (land and sea) seasonal energy-balance model per 2° latitude band, after North & Coakley (1979): Earth's outgoing-radiation fit (203.3 + 2.09·T W/m²), Earth's cloud albedo without snow or sea ice, diffusive poleward heat transport (0.649 W/m²/K), and heat capacities and land–sea exchange fitted to Earth's seasonal cycle. On Earth's orbit it gives a 15 °C global mean and −7 / 23 °C (January / July) on land at 45°N. Elevation comes from the Mars Global Surveyor MOLA gridded topography (MEGDR, 4 pixels per degree; Smith et al. 2003, NASA PDS Geosciences Node), packed into `mola-4ppd.txt` (5 m steps, row-delta coded, zlib-compressed, base64). Everything runs in the browser from `index.html` and that one data file, with no build step and no server.
