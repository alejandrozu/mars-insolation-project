Guide · the default three-sun world

How it works

The Mars Insolation Project imagines Mars after terraforming: Earth-like air at 1.05 atm, an ocean in the northern lowlands, seas in Hellas and Argyre, lakes in thousands of craters, and two giant mirror arrays that add their light to the Sun's. The simulator works out that world's days, sunlight, temperatures, rain and rivers. This guide goes through every choice behind its default world, the three-sun sky: what each one is, how it works, and why it was made.

The idea

Today Mars is a frozen desert under a thin carbon-dioxide atmosphere, about 0.6% of Earth's surface pressure. The project does not simulate how to turn it into a living planet. It assumes the big pieces are already in place and asks what the climate would then be:

  • Air like Earth's, at 1.05 atm at sea level.
  • Enough water for an ocean in the northern lowlands, with lakes wherever the land can hold them.
  • Extra light from mirrors, because Mars's own sunlight cannot keep such a world warm.

The last point is the heart of the project. With Earth-like air and only the Sun, the model puts the land between 45°S and 45°N at about −45 °C on average at sea level, and the ocean would freeze over. Every other choice (where the mirrors sit, how their light is shaped, how high the sea stands) follows from making that world comfortable for people.

The climate models behind the numbers were built and tested on Earth, fitted to its observed temperatures, winds, rain and lakes, and then run with Mars's orbit, spin, gravity, air and terrain. They show the climate this sunlight would drive. They are not a weather forecast.

Sunlight on Mars

Mars is 1.52 times farther from the Sun than Earth, so it gets 43% of Earth's sunlight: 589 W/m² above the air on average, against 1,361 W/m². Its orbit is also more elongated, with an eccentricity of 0.093 against Earth's 0.017, so its sunlight swings by 45% over the year, from 490 W/m² at aphelion to 713 W/m² at perihelion.

The three suns raise the yearly average to 1,458 W/m², 7% more than Earth gets. The chart shows where that light lands over a year. The Sun alone gives the familiar pattern, strongest at the equator and weakest at the poles. L5 adds most of its light away from the latitudes the Sun already heats, so that between 45°S and 45°N the Sun and L5 together give a nearly even 300–335 W/m². Beyond about 50° L4 adds a ring of extra light, peaking near 600 W/m² around 60°N and 460 W/m² around 60°S.

Why so much light at high latitudes? Temperature is the goal, not an even light map. High latitudes lose most of their light for months in winter, the slanting light crosses more air and cloud, and the winds carry heat away toward the poles. To end up only a little cooler than the tropics, they need more light over a year. Earth shows the opposite extreme: its equator gets 2.4 times what its poles get, which is why its poles are icy.

The three-sun sky

The default sky has three suns: the real Sun; a second sun at L5 that lights the whole planet with a shaped beam; and a supplemental sun at L4 that lights only the high latitudes. The populated band between 45°S and 45°N sees just the Sun and L5, so it keeps a normal rhythm of days and nights.

Why L4 and L5

L4 and L5 are two of the five Lagrange points of the Sun–Mars pair, on Mars's orbit 60° ahead of and 60° behind the planet. Each forms an equilateral triangle with the Sun and Mars. An object placed there keeps its place relative to both, and the points are stable: a small drift does not grow. Mars already has a few Trojan asteroids at these points, such as 5261 Eureka at L5.

Three things make them the right place for the mirrors:

  • They are as far from the Sun as Mars. The light a mirror catches, and so the light it sends on, rises and falls with Mars's own sunlight over the eccentric year. The mirror light is always the same multiple of sunlight.
  • They hold still in Mars's sky relative to the Sun. Seen from Mars, L4 always stands 60° west of the Sun along the ecliptic and L5 60° east. Mars turns 360° in a sol, so L4 rises and sets 4 Mars hours (4 h 07 min) before the Sun, a morning sun, and L5 4 Mars hours after it, an evening sun.
  • They are stable, so the arrays need only small corrections to stay in place.

In the simulatorSky bar → Three suns. L5 light and L4 light set each array's strength; Shaping switches between the two arrays' settings; Reset returns the whole sky to these defaults.

L5, the second sun

L5 sends 1.17 times the Sun's light, averaged over Mars, but not evenly. Its beam is shaped like a concave lens: dimmest along one line across the planet and brighter toward the north and south. The profile is

light ∝ 1 − k · cos 2(a − p), k = 0.85

divided by its own average so the total stays 1.17×. Here a is the latitude as seen from the array (the real latitude when the array is over the equator), p is the latitude of the dimmest line and k sets the depth of the dip. With k = 0.85 the dimmest line gets 0.26 times the average and the far north and south edges 3.2 times.

The dimmest line moves with the seasons:

p = 7° + 27.5° · sin(Ls − 8°) + 12° · sin 2(Ls − 54°)

It reaches 46°N in northern summer and 13°S at its southernmost, in northern autumn. It follows the latitude where the Sun stands overhead, and in northern summer runs well north of it, so the dimmest part of the beam falls where the Sun is already high and the days are long, and the mirror light goes where the Sun is weak: the winter hemisphere and the far summer latitudes. The second term, a twice-a-year swing, lets the two halves of the year differ, because Mars's eccentric orbit makes the southern summer short and hot and the northern summer long and mild.

Why shape the beam at all? A uniform second sun would simply multiply the Sun's own pattern, and the tropics would overheat long before the high latitudes warmed up. The lens moves light from latitudes that have too much to latitudes that have too little, which evens out both the yearly temperatures and the seasons.

In the simulatorUnder Shaping → L5, the light is set to Lens with k = 0.85 and a phase that follows the seasons; the Fine-tune box holds the bias, shift and twice-a-year terms.

L4, the supplemental sun

L4 lights only the outer latitudes: north of a northern edge and south of a southern edge, neither ever closer to the equator than 45°. Its strength is set at 0.51 times the Sun's, and over the year it delivers 0.31 times on average. Inside each cap the light rises from zero at the edge over a 9° ramp and then leans toward the edge rather than the pole (pole lean −0.9), falling to a tenth of its edge value at the pole itself. Its light peaks around 60° in each hemisphere, over the land around the northern ocean and the southern uplands, and tapers toward poles that are dark half the year anyway.

Four settings, given at Ls 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° and joined by smooth curves, steer it through the year:

Read across the table. In northern spring and autumn, L4 lights only the north, warming the northern ocean and the land beyond 50°N. In northern summer, which is the long southern winter near aphelion, it sends most of its light south to the southern uplands. Around perihelion, when Mars is closest to the Sun and the southern summer is at its hottest, it switches off.

No latitude gets more than 10 times the light the array would give it unshaped. Whatever a cap cannot take, in polar night for instance, goes to the other cap, so no light is wasted.

Why the north edge sits at 50–57°N rather than at the 45° limit: light delivered further poleward meets more cloud, and more of it is reflected back to space, so the same light warms the planet a little less. With the edge beyond 50°N and the north share set so that the northern cap still gets the light it needs, the populated band runs about 0.4 °C cooler.

Why a separate supplemental sun at all? The high latitudes need far more light per year than one shaped beam can give them without overheating the middle band (the two-sun sky below shows the result). L4 decouples the two problems: the caps get what they need, and the band keeps its nights.

In the simulatorUnder Shaping → L4, the light is set to Outer latitudes, with Soft edge 9° and Pole lean −0.9. The Seasons box holds the table above.

UV-free mirrors

The arrays reflect nothing shorter than 400 nm. Ultraviolet is 8% of sunlight above the air (6.3% UV-A, 1.3% UV-B, 0.5% UV-C). The mirrors make up for it by reflecting 1.087 times as much of the rest, so each still delivers its full stated light, 42% of it visible.

The reason is exposure. Full-spectrum mirrors would add 1.5 times the Sun's ultraviolet on top of the Sun's own, from two more directions and at hours when the Sun is low. With UV-free mirrors only the Sun sends ultraviolet. The clear-sky UV index at noon in the tropics, at sea level, is then about 2.5–3.5, against 10–12 in Earth's tropics: low to moderate on the WHO scale. Mars's thick air column helps too: under its weak gravity 1.05 atm means 2.77 times Earth's air overhead, which by itself cuts the index to 0.54 times.

The swap also warms the climate a little. In the stratosphere, ozone would have absorbed the mirrors' UV-B and UV-C, and air scatters near-ultraviolet light back to space more than visible light. The replacement visible and infrared light mostly reaches the lower air and the ground instead. A simple column model puts the effect at that of 2% more mirror light: about 1.3 °C warmer land and 9% more rain with three suns.

There are side effects. The stratosphere, warmed only by the Sun's ultraviolet, would be colder than Earth's. The mirrors' visible light breaks ozone down without the UV-C that makes it, so the ozone layer could be thinner than assumed. Plants use 400–700 nm and lose nothing they need; skin makes vitamin D from UV-B, which the Sun alone would supply at about a quarter of Earth's tropical UV index.

In the simulatorThe UV-free mirrors switch in the sky bar (on by default). Every insolation view can show All light, Visible or the UV index.

How the settings were chosen

The numbers above come from a search, not from intuition. Candidate skies were run through the full climate model with the default air, water and lakes, and scored against comfort targets for the people, the sea and the far north and south:

  • land between 45°S and 45°N with a daily mean between 0 and 30 °C;
  • the ocean north of the equator between 20 and 30 °C;
  • land poleward of 45° between −20 and 30 °C.

The best skies keep as many sols as possible inside those ranges with all the mirror light delivered. Among skies that do about equally well, the default favours more rain over land and less arid land, meaning land that gets under 300 mm a year, roughly the edge of dry country. The results are below.

What it would take to build

The simulator treats each array as a light source with a set strength and shape. Building one is a different matter. These are order-of-magnitude estimates, outside the simulator:

  • Size. A mirror cannot be brighter than the Sun it reflects. To deliver 1.17 times the Sun's light, the L5 array has to fill at least 1.17 times the Sun's apparent area in Mars's sky. L5 is as far from Mars as the Sun is, so the array must be roughly as large as the Sun itself: at least 1.5 million km across, a reflecting area of about 2 × 1012 km² tilted 30° to the sunlight, and more to shape the beam. L4, which concentrates its light on the caps, is of the same order.
  • Mass. Reflective film weighing a few grams per square metre adds up to about 1016 kg, the material of an asteroid 20–30 km across.
  • Aiming. From 228 million km away, even a perfect mirror spreads the Sun's image into a patch of light about 1.4 million km wide, 200 times wider than Mars, so almost all of each facet's light misses. To shape the light, each facet is aimed so the sharp edge of its sun-image falls across Mars, lighting the planet on one side of a line and not the other. Many facets with their edges at different latitudes build up any profile, the lens of L5 or the caps of L4.
  • Light pressure. Sunlight pushes on a mirror. For film of 3 g/m² the push is about 40% of the Sun's pull, so the arrays would sit somewhat off the classical L4 and L5 points and need constant steering, unless the film is heavier: at about 25 g/m² the push drops under 5%.

The two-sun alternative

The simulator also offers a two-sun sky: one array at L5 sending 1.33 times the Sun's light, which brings Mars's average to about Earth's. Its lens is deeper (k = 1, swinging by ±0.2 over the year), with the dimmest line swinging ±27.5° around 11.5°N.

It is a much harder world. With one beam doing all the shaping, warming the high latitudes enough would overheat the middle, so the compromise leaves the populated band below 0 °C on about 29 sols a year, the northern ocean at about 10 °C, 330 mm of rain over land with 61% of the land under 300 mm, and Hellas shrunk to a lake 2 km deep on its floor. That is why the three-sun sky is the default.

Air

The default sea-level pressure is 1.05 atm, with an Earth-like mix of nitrogen and oxygen.

Pressure is the weight of the air overhead. Mars's gravity is 0.38 of Earth's, so 1 atm on Mars takes 2.64 times as much air above each square metre, and 1.05 atm takes 2.77 times. In total that is about 70% of the mass of Earth's atmosphere, spread over a planet with 28% of Earth's surface.

1.05 atm was chosen so that people can breathe it comfortably and the highlands stay livable. Under weak gravity pressure falls slowly with height, so the southern highlands, on average 3.7 km above the sea, still have about 0.89 atm.

Consequences the simulator includes:

  • Earth-equivalent heights. With the same air, pressure falls 0.38 times as fast with height as on Earth. Heights can be shown in Earth-equivalent km (Mars km × 0.38), the climb that would thin the air as much on Earth. Olympus Mons, about 24 km above the sea, is like a 9.2 km climb on Earth.
  • More heat stored and carried. The air that takes part in the seasons stores heat, so the land's heat capacity grows with the air above it. Heat transport scales with pressure and with the square of the planet's spin rate (Williams & Kasting 1997), which comes to about Earth's at 1 atm.
  • Smaller daily swings, more water vapour per column and a lower UV index, covered in their own sections.

The model keeps Earth's greenhouse effect. With 2.6 times Earth's air at Earth's composition the greenhouse would be stronger, so the composition is assumed adjusted to give Earth-like warming.

Getting thereMars's own carbon dioxide could add only a few hundredths of an atmosphere, and the planet has little nitrogen, so most of this air would have to come from elsewhere. The project takes the air as given.

Water

Sea level is set by the share of Mars's surface that lies below it. The default, 26%, puts the sea 3.12 km below the MOLA datum, Mars's reference level. Only water joined to the northern lowlands stands at that level: an ocean covering 23.8% of the planet, about 1 km deep on average and 3.8 km at its deepest.

Hellas and Argyre, the two giant impact basins of the south, reach below that level but are walled in, so they are closed seas with their own levels, like the Caspian. Every other closed depression, from large basins to small craters, holds a lake.

How full each lake is comes from its own water balance. A lake rises until evaporation from its surface equals what flows in: the runoff from its catchment, the rain on it and the overflow from basins upstream. A basin that fills spills through its lowest point into the next basin or the sea. The lakes change the rain and the temperatures in turn, so the balance is repeated until they settle.

With three suns the result is a wet planet:

  • Hellas fills to its brim and spills. It becomes a sea 9.3 km deep whose surface stands 1.27 km above the datum, 4.4 km above the ocean. Once full it evaporates about 0.48 m a year and gets about 0.49 m of rain, so it sustains itself, as Earth's oceans do at the same latitudes.
  • Argyre fills and spills too, 5.1 km deep, its surface 0.14 km above the datum.
  • The crater lakes are 84–95% full on average, depending on the region, and about 84% of them spill.
  • Water covers 45% of the planet: ocean 23.8%, lakes and closed seas 21.4% (Hellas and Argyre 7.2% of it). That leaves 54.8% land.
  • In all, a global layer of 475 m: 229 m in the ocean, 202 m in Hellas and Argyre and 44 m in the other lakes.

Why 26%: it turns the northern lowlands into a real ocean while leaving more than half the planet as land once the lakes have filled. The lakes and the southern seas then put open water across the southern highlands, far from the ocean, which feeds rain there.

Getting there475 m is 69 million km³, about 5% of Earth's oceans. Mars's polar caps hold enough ice for a layer of roughly 20–30 m and more is frozen in the ground; the rest would have to be brought in, for example from icy bodies of the outer solar system. The project takes the water as given.

In the simulatorMap → Water level; Fill craters and closed basins with Lakes: Water balance; Hellas, Argyre: Own level. The readout gives the water needed.

How the climate is computed

Every climate number comes from models built and tested on Earth, then run with Mars's orbit, spin, gravity, air and terrain.

Temperature

The temperatures come from an energy-balance model of the kind climate scientists use for first estimates (Budyko–Sellers type, after North & Coakley 1979). Each place absorbs sunlight with Earth's cloud albedo, radiates heat to space following Earth's measured relation (203.3 + 2.09·T W/m², with T in °C), stores heat in land and sea, and passes heat toward the poles. Its heat capacities and exchanges were fitted to Earth's seasons: on Earth's orbit it gives Earth's 15 °C global mean and the right winters and summers at 45°N.

The default dynamic setting runs it on a 4° × 6° grid with the map's own ocean, seas and lakes, and with Earth-like prevailing winds (trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies) carrying heat east or west. Fitted to 23 mid-latitude cities on Earth, it reproduces their January–July temperature range within 4.3 K.

For Mars, the land's heat capacity includes the deeper soil layer that Mars's longer year reaches, plus the extra air; heat transport scales with pressure and spin rate. The model has no snow, sea ice or clouds that change with the climate, so read it as how warm the light makes each place.

Cooling with height

Air cools as you climb. The default lapse rate is 6 K per Earth-equivalent km, which is 2.3 K per Mars km. It is the middle of what mountain stations on Earth measure for daily mean temperatures (about 4.5 K/km in the Pyrenees, 5.5 on Mount Washington, from 4.4 in winter to 8.2 in summer in the Alps, 6.9 in the Ecuadorian Andes) and of humid air cooling as it rises (5.5–5.7 K/km over a year). The often-quoted 6.5 K/km is the standard atmosphere's average for the free air up to 11 km, not for the ground.

The rate is given per Earth-equivalent km because rising air cools with the drop in pressure, and under Mars gravity pressure drops 0.38 times as fast per km. Each patch of land is cooled from its sea-level temperature by its own height, and so is each lake: Hellas's surface, 4.4 km above the ocean, is about 10 K cooler than the ocean at the same latitude.

Day and night

The energy balance gives each sol's average. A second model gives the daily high and low: it treats the ground and the air near it as one layer, warmed by the light of all the suns over the sol and losing heat in proportion to how warm it is. It was fitted to typical daily ranges on Earth's land, from 16 K in desert summers and 9.5 K in the humid tropics to 5.5 K in maritime winters, with the high near 14:30.

For Mars it takes in the 2.7% longer sol and the thick air column, which narrows the daily range to 0.67 times Earth's for the same sunlight and humidity. Three suns stretch the warm part of the day, since the morning sun rises before the Sun and the evening sun sets after it, so daily ranges are mostly 4–7 K.

In the simulatorOn the map, Map: Insolation and Show: Insolation-based temperature. When: chosen date, yearly average, seasonal swing, 10th warmest or 10th coldest sol. Each sol: average, high or low. The Lapse rate slider sits below.

Rain

Rain comes from a moisture budget on a 2° grid. Water evaporates from the ocean, the lakes and wet ground. Low-level winds, driven by the temperature contrasts, carry the vapour. It rains where air rises or the column nears saturation, on windward slopes and along storm tracks, and high terrain holds moist air back. The year is solved in 12 slices, twice, the second time with the soil water the first pass leaves.

It was tested on Earth with the same code and Earth's radius, rotation, gravity and terrain. Given Earth's observed temperatures and winds, its rain over land correlates 0.85 with the observed and its global total is within 4% (1,020 mm a year against 983). Run as it runs for Mars, from its own temperatures and winds, it does worse: a correlation of 0.63, typically within a factor of 2. Its summer monsoons come out too weak and it fills Earth's large closed basins too full (it fills the Caspian basin, for instance). On Mars that means the monsoon rain on the southern highlands is probably underestimated, and full seas in Hellas and Argyre are the wet end of what is possible.

Rivers and lakes

Rivers follow the yearly runoff (rain minus what the land evaporates) downhill. Each lake gathers its inflow and passes water on only once it is full. Where a river runs through a chain of lakes it is drawn straight across each one, from where it enters to the spill point, so it reads as one river. Flows above about 25 m³/s are drawn, much wider than real so they show at the scale of a planet.

What is left out

  • Ocean currents. The heat the oceans carry poleward is included on average, spread evenly over land and sea, but there are no gyres, warm boundary currents like the Gulf Stream, upwelling or deep circulation.
  • Snow, sea ice and changing clouds, and the feedbacks they bring.
  • Mountains blocking heat transport. Rain does see terrain; temperatures only through height.
  • Dust, soils and vegetation.
  • Time. How the planet got here, and how long its air and water would last, are outside the model.

Martian quirks

Mars looks Earth-like at first glance: a day of almost 24 hours and a similar tilt. The differences behind that resemblance shape every part of the project.

Orbit and calendar

490–713 W/m²

A lopsided orbit

Mars's distance from the Sun runs from 1.38 to 1.67 AU, so its sunlight swings by 45% over the year; Earth's swings by 7%. Because the mirrors share Mars's distance from the Sun, their light swings with it.

193 · 179 · 143 · 154 sols

Unequal seasons

Northern spring, summer, autumn and winter. Perihelion comes at Ls 251°, just before the northern winter solstice, so the southern summer is short and hot and the southern winter long and cold. The three-sun sky is built around it: L4 switches off at perihelion and sends its light south through the long southern winter.

Ls 0°–360°

A calendar by angle

Mars's seasons are counted by the solar longitude, Ls: the angle Mars has moved round the Sun since the northern spring equinox. Ls 90° is the northern summer solstice, 180° the autumn equinox, 270° the winter solstice. Equal steps of Ls take unequal time.

24 h 39 m 35 s

A slightly longer day

A sol is 2.7% longer than an Earth day. The simulator's clocks use Mars hours, a 24th of a sol (1.0275 Earth hours), on local solar time, so 12:00 is when the Sun crosses the meridian.

668.6 sols

A long year

A Martian year lasts 687 Earth days, 1.88 Earth years. Each season lasts about twice as long as on Earth, giving the ground more time to heat up and cool down, and the seasonal heat reaches deeper into the soil.

−51 to +40 min

Sundials and clocks disagree

Because of the eccentric orbit, a Martian sundial runs up to about 50 minutes ahead of or behind a steady clock over the year. On Earth the difference is at most 16 minutes.

The sky

0.35°

A smaller, fainter Sun

From Mars the Sun is about two-thirds as wide as from Earth (0.53°) and gives 43% of the light.

05:40 → 23:42

Three suns, long days

The morning sun at L4 rises 4 Mars hours before the Sun and the evening sun at L5 sets 4 Mars hours after it. At 45°N in northern spring there is light from 05:40 to 23:42. Each array looks at least as wide as the Sun, since it can be no brighter. From the populated band L4 would look dark, its light aimed at the caps.

2 moons

A moon that rises in the west

Phobos circles Mars faster than Mars turns, so it rises in the west and sets in the east, twice a sol. Deimos rises in the east and takes about two and a half sols to set. Neither is in the simulator.

5261 Eureka

Neighbours at L5

Mars's Lagrange points already hold a few Trojan asteroids, most of them at L5, the second sun's home.

25.19°

A wandering tilt

Mars's tilt is close to Earth's 23.44° today, but with no large moon to steady it, it has swung between about 15° and 45° over the last 10 million years. The sky is designed for today's tilt.

Gravity and air

2.77× the air

Heavy air columns

Mars's gravity is 0.38 g, so 1.05 atm needs 2.77 times Earth's air above each square metre. Pressure falls 2.6 times more slowly with height: at the same temperature the air thins by a factor e every 22 km, against 8.4 km on Earth.

× 0.38

Earth-equivalent heights

Multiplying Mars heights by 0.38 gives the Earth climb with the same drop in pressure. Olympus Mons, 24 km above the sea, thins the air like a 9.2 km climb on Earth, and the highlands at 3.7 km keep 0.89 atm.

2.6× the vapour

More water in the air

The deeper air column holds 2.6 times as much water vapour as Earth's at the same temperature, which matters for how much rain a storm can carry.

0.67× the swing

Gentler days and nights

More air overhead damps the daily cycle: for the same sunlight and humidity, the range between day and night is two-thirds of Earth's. Today's thin Martian air swings by 50–70 K.

UV index 2.5–3.5

A weak sunburn

The fainter Sun, the heavy air column and UV-free mirrors keep the tropical noon UV index at sea level a quarter of Earth's. Sunscreen is optional; vitamin D comes slowly.

Land and water

30 km of relief

Highest and lowest

From the floor of Hellas, about 8 km below the datum, to the top of Olympus Mons, about 21 km above it, Mars's relief spans almost 30 km. Earth's, from the Mariana Trench to Everest, spans 20.

North low, south high

A planet of two halves

The northern third of Mars lies several kilometres lower than the cratered southern highlands. That is why the ocean fills the north and the south is a high continent dotted with lakes.

+4.4 km

Seas above the sea

Full Hellas stands 4.4 km above the ocean and is 9.3 km deep, a sea perched on the highlands, like the Caspian lifted onto a plateau. Its water is about 10 K cooler than the ocean at the same latitude.

14% lakes

A planet of lakes

Besides Hellas and Argyre, crater lakes cover about 14% of Mars. Thousands of old impact craters become lakes, many spilling into the next.

Weather and climate

1.4× wider

Wider tropics

The tropical circulation cells reach about 1.4 times as far from the equator as Earth's, by the scaling for how far they can extend, which depends on the planet's size, spin and temperature contrasts.

26 °C at 80°N

Warmest in the far north

With three suns the land averages about 21–22 °C over the year (at sea level) from 60°S to 45°N, and the warmest land is the far north, around 26 °C beyond 60°N, where L4's light lands. The tropics are not the warmest latitudes, so they get their rain from circulation cells crossing the equator rather than from an Earth-like tropical rain belt.

No ice caps

Temperate poles

With L4 lighting the caps, land north of 45°N never has a sol averaging below −20 °C and land south of 45°S has about one a year. Winter snow could still fall in places, but away from the highest volcano summits nowhere is cold enough for an ice cap. The model itself has no ice.

Using the simulator

This link opens the simulator with the three-sun sky. Each panel has a Reset button that returns it to the defaults described here.

  • Sky bar. Natural, Two suns or Three suns; the UV-free switch; each array's strength and shaping.
  • Mars view. The orbit to scale, or a ground view of the sky dome at any latitude with every sun's path; drag Mars round its orbit to change the date.
  • Daylight, Insolation and Ratio. Day length, sunlight (daily mean or noon peak) and the seasonal swing across the year, for any set of latitudes.
  • Map. Water level, lakes and seas; elevation or insolation layers showing sunlight (all light, visible or UV index), temperature or rain; rivers; height and depth lines; a flat map that wraps round or a 3D globe.
  • Temperature. Temperature by latitude over the land, with the counts of cold and hot sols used in this guide.
