Guide · the default three-sun world

How it works, and why

The Mars Insolation Project imagines Mars after terraforming: Earth-like air at 1.05 atm, an ocean in the northern lowlands, seas in Hellas and Argyre, lakes in thousands of craters, and two giant mirror arrays that add their light to the Sun's. The simulator works out that world's days, sunlight, temperatures, rain, rivers and seasons. This guide sets out every choice behind its default world and why it was made, then how the model computes each step.

Part I

The choices, and why

Every default in the simulator is a decision. Each section here opens with the decision itself: what was chosen, the reason, and what it was chosen over. The reasoning follows. Everything goes back to one goal, set out first.

01The goal

ChosenA Mars where people could live under open sky: a mild climate across the populated band, 45°S–45°N, a warm sea, and far north and south that are cold in winter but not frozen.
BecauseEvery other choice (where the mirrors sit, how their light is shaped, how much air and water there is) only matters for the climate it produces. A clear target makes those choices testable.
Instead ofSimulating how to terraform Mars. The project assumes the big pieces are already in place and asks what the climate would then be.

Today Mars is a frozen desert under a thin carbon-dioxide atmosphere, about 0.6% of Earth's surface pressure. The project takes three things as given:

  • Air like Earth's, at 1.05 atm at sea level.
  • Enough water for an ocean in the northern lowlands, with lakes wherever the land can hold them.
  • Extra light from mirrors, because Mars's own sunlight cannot keep such a world warm.

The climate is then judged against three comfort targets, all on daily mean temperatures at each place's own height:

  • Land between 45°S and 45°N between 0 and 30 °C, roughly the span of Earth's temperate and subtropical climates, on as many sols as possible.
  • The ocean north of the equator between 20 and 30 °C, a warm sea like Earth's subtropical oceans, which also feeds the rain.
  • Land poleward of 45° between −20 and 30 °C: cold winters are fine, an ice-cap climate is not.

Candidate skies were run through the full climate model with the default air, water and lakes, and scored on how many sols they keep inside those ranges with all the mirror light delivered. Among skies that did about equally well, the default favours more rain over land and less arid land, meaning land that gets under 300 mm a year, roughly the edge of dry country. Section 10 shows how the result scores.

The climate models behind every number were built and tested on Earth, fitted to its observed temperatures, winds, rain and lakes, and then run with Mars's orbit, spin, gravity, air and terrain (Part II). They show the climate this sunlight would drive. They are not a weather forecast.

02Why add light

ChosenAdd sunlight with mirrors until Mars gets 1,458 W/m² on average above the air, 107% of what Earth gets.
BecauseWith Earth-like air and the Sun alone, the model puts the land between 45°S and 45°N at about −45 °C on average at sea level, and the ocean would freeze over.
Instead ofA much stronger greenhouse. The model holds the greenhouse at Earth's strength, so the extra warmth has to arrive as light.

Mars is 1.52 times farther from the Sun than Earth, so it gets 43% of Earth's sunlight: 589 W/m² above the air on average, against 1,361 W/m². Its orbit is also more elongated, with an eccentricity of 0.093 against Earth's 0.017, so its sunlight swings by 45% over the year, from 490 W/m² at aphelion to 713 W/m² at perihelion.

The three suns raise the yearly average to 1,458 W/m². The chart shows where that light lands. The Sun alone gives the familiar pattern, strongest at the equator and weakest at the poles. L5 adds most of its light away from the latitudes the Sun already heats, so that between 45°S and 45°N the Sun and L5 together give a nearly even 300–335 W/m². Beyond about 50° L4 adds a ring of extra light, peaking near 600 W/m² around 60°N and 460 W/m² around 60°S.

Why so much light at high latitudes? The goal is temperature, not an even light map. High latitudes lose most of their light for months in winter, the slanting light crosses more air and cloud, and the winds carry heat away toward the poles. To end up only a little cooler than the tropics, they need more light over a year. Earth shows the opposite extreme: its equator gets 2.4 times what its poles get, which is why its poles are icy.

03Mirrors at L4 and L5

ChosenTwo mirror arrays at the Sun–Mars Lagrange points L4 and L5, 60° ahead of and behind Mars on its orbit.
BecauseThey are as far from the Sun as Mars, they hold still in Mars's sky relative to the Sun, and they are stable.
Instead ofMirrors orbiting Mars itself, which would pass through Mars's shadow and sweep their light across the planet as they circle it.

L4 and L5 are two of the five Lagrange points of the Sun–Mars pair. Each forms an equilateral triangle with the Sun and Mars. An object placed there keeps its place relative to both, and the points are stable: a small drift does not grow. Mars already has a few Trojan asteroids at these points, such as 5261 Eureka at L5.

  • They are as far from the Sun as Mars. The light a mirror catches, and so the light it sends on, rises and falls with Mars's own sunlight over the eccentric year. The mirror light is always the same multiple of sunlight.
  • They hold still in Mars's sky relative to the Sun. Seen from Mars, L4 always stands 60° west of the Sun along the ecliptic and L5 60° east. Mars turns 360° in a sol, so L4 rises and sets about 4 Mars hours before the Sun, a morning sun, and L5 about 4 Mars hours after it, an evening sun.
  • They are stable, so the arrays need only small corrections to stay in place.

The two arrays get different jobs. L5 lights the whole planet with a shaped beam (section 04). L4 lights only the high latitudes (section 05), so the populated band sees just the Sun and L5 and keeps a normal rhythm of days and nights.

In the simulatorSky bar → Three suns. L5 light and L4 light set each array's strength; Shaping switches between the two arrays' settings; Reset returns the whole sky to these defaults.

04L5, the second sun

Chosen1.17 times the Sun's light, in a beam shaped like a concave lens (depth k = 0.85) whose dimmest line moves with the seasons.
BecauseAn even second sun would simply multiply the Sun's own pattern: the tropics would overheat long before the high latitudes warmed up.
Instead ofAn even beam, or a fixed lens that ignores the seasons.

The beam is dimmest along one line across the planet and brighter toward the north and south. The profile is

light ∝ 1 − k · cos 2(a − p), k = 0.85

divided by its own average so the total stays 1.17×. Here a is the latitude as seen from the array, p the latitude of the dimmest line and k the depth of the dip. With k = 0.85 the dimmest line gets 0.26 times the average and the far north and south edges 3.2 times. Section 16 gives the details.

The dimmest line moves with the seasons:

p = 7° + 27.5° · sin(Ls − 8°) + 12° · sin 2(Ls − 54°)

It reaches 46°N in northern summer and 13°S at its southernmost, in northern autumn. It follows the latitude where the Sun stands overhead, and in northern summer runs well north of it, so the dimmest part of the beam falls where the Sun is already high and the days are long. The mirror light goes where the Sun is weak: the winter hemisphere and the far summer latitudes. The twice-a-year term lets the two halves of the year differ, because Mars's eccentric orbit makes the southern summer short and hot and the northern summer long and mild.

In the simulatorUnder Shaping → L5: Lens with k = 0.85 and a phase that follows the seasons; the Fine-tune box holds the bias, shift and twice-a-year terms.

05L4, the supplemental sun

ChosenLight only for the outer latitudes, beyond edges that never come inside 45°: up to 0.51 times the Sun's light, 0.31 on average, steered between north and south through the year.
BecauseThe high latitudes need far more light a year than one shaped beam can give them without overheating the middle. A separate array solves the two problems separately, and the band keeps its nights.
Instead ofOne stronger L5 doing all the work: the two-sun sky, which leaves a much harder world (section 10).

Inside each cap the light rises from zero at the edge over a 9° ramp and then leans toward the edge rather than the pole (pole lean −0.9), falling to a tenth of its edge value at the pole itself. It peaks around 60° in each hemisphere, over the land around the northern ocean and the southern uplands, and tapers toward poles that are dark half the year anyway.

Four settings, given at Ls 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° and joined by smooth curves, steer it through the year:

Ls 0° · N springLs 90° · N summerLs 180° · N autumnLs 270° · N winter
North edge51°N51°N57°N50°N
South edge47°S48°S69°S72°S
Share sent north100%16%100%31%
Strength69%100%57%0%

Read across the table. In northern spring and autumn, L4 lights only the north, warming the northern ocean and the land beyond 50°N. In northern summer, which is the long southern winter near aphelion, it sends most of its light south to the southern uplands. Around perihelion, when Mars is closest to the Sun and the southern summer is at its hottest, it switches off.

No latitude gets more than 10 times the light the array would give it unshaped, and whatever a cap cannot take, in polar night for instance, goes to the other cap, so no light is wasted.

Why the north edge sits at 50–57°N rather than at the 45° limit: light delivered further poleward meets more cloud, and more of it is reflected back to space, so the same light warms the planet a little less. With the edge beyond 50°N, and the north share set so that the northern cap still gets the light it needs, the populated band runs about 0.4 °C cooler.

In the simulatorUnder Shaping → L4: Outer latitudes, Soft edge 9° and Pole lean −0.9. The Seasons box holds the table above.

06UV-free mirrors

ChosenMirrors that reflect nothing shorter than 400 nm, and 1.087 times as much of the rest, so each still delivers its full stated light.
BecauseOnly the Sun then sends ultraviolet, and the tropical noon UV index at sea level stays at 2.5–3.5, against 10–12 on Earth.
Instead ofFull-spectrum mirrors, which would add 1.5 times the Sun's ultraviolet on top of the Sun's own, from two more directions and at hours when the Sun is low.

Ultraviolet is 8% of sunlight above the air (6.3% UV-A, 1.3% UV-B, 0.5% UV-C). Without it the mirrors' light is 42% visible. Mars's thick air column helps too: under its weak gravity 1.05 atm means 2.77 times Earth's air overhead, which by itself cuts the UV index to 0.54 times.

The swap also warms the climate a little. In the stratosphere, ozone would have absorbed the mirrors' UV-B and UV-C, and air scatters near-ultraviolet light back to space more than visible light. The replacement visible and infrared light mostly reaches the lower air and the ground instead. A simple column model puts the effect at that of 2% more mirror light: about 1.3 °C warmer land and 9% more rain with three suns.

There are side effects. The stratosphere, warmed only by the Sun's ultraviolet, would be colder than Earth's. The mirrors' visible light breaks ozone down without the UV-C that makes it, so the ozone layer could be thinner than assumed. Plants use 400–700 nm and lose nothing they need; skin makes vitamin D from UV-B, which the Sun alone would supply at about a quarter of Earth's tropical UV index.

In the simulatorThe UV-free mirrors switch in the sky bar (on by default). Every insolation view can show All light, Visible or the UV index.

07Air

Chosen1.05 atm at sea level, with an Earth-like mix of nitrogen and oxygen.
BecausePeople breathe it comfortably at sea level, and under Mars's weak gravity pressure falls slowly with height, so the southern highlands, on average 3.7 km above the sea, still have about 0.89 atm.
Instead ofThe same mass of air as Earth's, which under Mars gravity would give only 0.38 atm.

Pressure is the weight of the air overhead. Mars's gravity is 0.38 of Earth's, so 1 atm on Mars takes 2.64 times as much air above each square metre, and 1.05 atm takes 2.77 times. In total that is about 70% of the mass of Earth's atmosphere, spread over a planet with 28% of Earth's surface.

Consequences the simulator includes:

  • Earth-equivalent heights. With the same air, pressure falls 0.38 times as fast with height as on Earth. Heights can be shown in Earth-equivalent km (Mars km × 0.38), the climb that would thin the air as much on Earth. Olympus Mons, about 24 km above the sea, is like a 9.2 km climb on Earth.
  • More heat stored and carried. The air that takes part in the seasons stores heat, so the land's heat capacity grows with the air above it, and heat transport scales with pressure (section 18).
  • Smaller daily swings, more water vapour per column and a lower UV index (sections 21, 22 and 17).

The model keeps Earth's greenhouse effect. With 2.6 times Earth's air at Earth's composition the greenhouse would be stronger, so the composition is assumed adjusted to give Earth-like warming.

Getting thereMars's own carbon dioxide could add only a few hundredths of an atmosphere, and the planet has little nitrogen, so most of this air would have to come from elsewhere. The project takes the air as given.

In the simulatorOn the map, with Show: Insolation-based temperature, the Sea-level pressure control; Heights: Earth-equivalent km for heights.

08Water

ChosenSea level at the height below which 26% of the surface lies (3.12 km below the MOLA datum), with every closed basin holding the lake its own water balance sustains.
BecauseThat turns the northern lowlands into a real ocean while leaving more than half the planet as land, and the lakes and southern seas spread open water across the highlands, far from the ocean, which feeds rain there.
Instead ofMore sea, or one fill level for every crater regardless of its climate (still available as Same fill everywhere).

Only water joined to the northern lowlands stands at sea level: an ocean covering 23.8% of the planet, about 1 km deep on average and 3.8 km at its deepest. Hellas and Argyre, the two giant impact basins of the south, reach below sea level but are walled in, so they are closed seas with their own levels, like the Caspian. Every other closed depression, from large basins to small craters, holds a lake.

How full each lake is comes from its own water balance: it rises until evaporation from its surface equals what flows in, from its catchment, the rain on it and the basins upstream (section 24).

With three suns the result is a wet planet:

  • Hellas fills to its brim and spills. It becomes a sea 9.3 km deep whose surface stands 1.27 km above the datum, 4.4 km above the ocean. Once full it evaporates about 0.48 m a year and gets about 0.49 m of rain, so it sustains itself, as Earth's oceans do at the same latitudes.
  • Argyre fills and spills too, 5.1 km deep, its surface 0.14 km above the datum.
  • The crater lakes are 84–95% full on average, depending on the region, and about 84% of them spill.
  • Water covers 45% of the planet: ocean 23.8%, lakes and closed seas 21.4% (Hellas and Argyre 7.2% of it).
  • In all, a global layer of 475 m: 229 m in the ocean, 202 m in Hellas and Argyre and 44 m in the other lakes.

Getting there475 m is 69 million km³, about 5% of Earth's oceans. Mars's polar caps hold enough ice for a layer of roughly 20–30 m and more is frozen in the ground; the rest would have to be brought in, for example from icy bodies of the outer solar system. The project takes the water as given.

In the simulatorMap → Water level; Fill craters and closed basins with Lakes: Water balance; Hellas, Argyre: Own level. The readout gives the water needed.

09Cooling with height

ChosenA lapse rate of 6 K per Earth-equivalent km, which is 2.3 K per Mars km.
BecauseIt is the middle of what mountain stations on Earth measure for daily mean temperatures, and of humid air cooling as it rises.
Instead ofThe often-quoted 6.5 K/km, the standard atmosphere's average for the free air up to 11 km rather than for the ground; or 5 K/km, which left high ground about 1 °C warmer per Earth-equivalent km.

Measured rates for daily means are about 4.5 K/km in the Pyrenees, 5.5 on Mount Washington, from 4.4 in winter to 8.2 in summer in the Alps, and 6.9 in the Ecuadorian Andes. Air at 85% humidity cooling along the moist adiabat gives 5.5–5.7 K/km over a year.

The rate is given per Earth-equivalent km because rising air cools with the drop in pressure, and under Mars gravity pressure drops 0.38 times as fast per km. Each patch of land is cooled from its sea-level temperature by its own height, and so is each lake: Hellas's surface, 4.4 km above the ocean, is about 10 K cooler than the ocean at the same latitude. With 6 rather than 5 K/km the band has about 6 more frosty sols a year, all of them on high ground.

In the simulatorThe Lapse rate slider under the temperature layer's settings.

The frosty sols in the populated band fall almost all on high ground, Tharsis, Elysium and the southern uplands, where the air is cooler. The far north is the warm spot: land beyond 45°N passes 30 °C on about 25 sols a year, the price of keeping the northern ocean warm and the populated band cool.

The two-sun alternative

The simulator also offers a two-sun sky: one array at L5 sending 1.33 times the Sun's light, which brings Mars's average to about Earth's. Its lens is deeper (k = 1, swinging by ±0.2 over the year), with the dimmest line swinging ±27.5° around 11.5°N. With one beam doing all the shaping, warming the high latitudes enough would overheat the middle, so the compromise leaves the populated band below 0 °C on about 29 sols a year, the northern ocean at about 10 °C, 330 mm of rain over land with 61% of the land under 300 mm, and Hellas shrunk to a lake 2 km deep on its floor. That is why the three-sun sky is the default.

In the simulatorThe Temperature panel (temperature by latitude) counts these sols; hover the map for any place's values.

11Five seasons

ChosenFive seasons: Autumn, Winter, Spring, Summer and Estia, named after the five traditional Spanish seasons (Estia, from estío, is the late, dry summer).
BecauseThey group the climate's real phases best: about 80% of the populated band's seasonal temperature changes, against 62% for the four astronomical seasons and 74% for the best four equal ones.
Instead ofEarth's four, which do not follow this climate, or six, which add little and split a stretch the band can barely tell apart.

Earth's four seasons work because, in the temperate zones, temperature follows one yearly wave set by the tilt, and the two hemispheres mirror each other. On the three-sun Mars that pattern breaks down. The mirrors were tuned to cancel most of the tilt's seasons, and what is left is the orbit's: 45% more sunlight at perihelion, and a slow passage through the far side of the orbit. The whole populated band follows that cycle together. The northern half is warmest around Ls 260°, its astronomical winter, and the southern half about 30° later.

So the seasons were found from the climate itself. The model's year was split into parts, with the start chosen so that each part groups the most similar sols of land temperature and rain between 45°S and 45°N (section 26 has the method). Going from four parts to five gains far more than going from five to six, and every version of the test puts the five boundaries in the same places, within a few degrees of Ls.

Temperatures are sea-level-equivalent land averages; the ground is cooler by about 2.3 K per km of height. The two halves of the band share the temperature seasons but not the rain: the north gets its rain in the cool months, like a Mediterranean climate, and the south in the warm ones, like a monsoon.

Why is Spring the cold season? The climate lags the light by about a season, because the ocean and the soil store heat. In the band, Autumn gets the least light of the year (about 295 W/m² on average against 393 in Summer) but stays mild on the heat stored through Summer and Estia; the cold arrives in Spring, when the light is already rising again. Spring is still a time of growing light, as on Earth.

Why are Summer and Estia shorter? Near perihelion Mars moves fastest and the climate changes fastest, so a season there needs fewer sols to hold one phase of the weather. The calendar (next section) makes Summer and Estia 127 sols and the other three 138, which fits the populated band's climate as well as equal seasons or slightly better.

12The calendar

ChosenA year that starts at Ls 0°, with 20 months, four in each season, made of 6-sol weeks: months of 36 sols (six weeks) or 30 (five), plus two festival sols, three in leap years.
BecauseEvery month is a whole number of weeks, every season a whole number of months, and the months add up to the seasons exactly. The start matches how Mars years are already counted.
Instead ofA 15-month year of 9-sol weeks, or the Darian calendar's 24 months of 27–28 sols, which was designed for a planet without this climate's seasons.

Why the year starts at Ls 0°

Ls 0° is the northern spring equinox. Planetary scientists number Mars years from it: Mars Year 1 began on 11 April 1955 (Clancy et al. 2000), and the Darian calendar also begins its year near it. For this climate it is a good place too: the best five-season split starts within a few degrees of it (Ls 7–9°), so starting the year there, and the first season with it, costs nothing in how well the seasons fit.

Months and weeks

A 6-sol week divides both month lengths, so each month begins on the first day of a week. Autumn, Winter and Spring are each 36 + 36 + 36 + 30 = 138 sols; Summer is 36 + 30 + 30 + 30 = 126 sols plus Perihelia, and Estia 126 plus Finalia (and Finalia II in leap years). In all, 111 weeks: 666 sols, plus the festivals.

Festival sols

The festivals sit outside the weeks, like the five or six complementary days at the end of the French Republican calendar or the five epagomenal days of the ancient Egyptian one, so they never break the weekly cycle:

  • Perihelia, the 31st of Calidor, five sols before perihelion, the year's closest approach to the Sun.
  • Finalia, the 31st of Finival, the last sol of the year, and in leap years Finalia II, the 32nd.

Leap years

A year has 668 sols, or 669 in a leap year. Odd years and years divisible by 10 are leap years, except centuries not divisible by 500. Over 1,000 years that gives 592 leap years, an average of 668.592 sols, against 668.59 for the real Martian year from one northern spring equinox to the next.

13Building the mirrors

The simulator treats each array as a light source with a set strength and shape. Building one is a different matter. These are order-of-magnitude estimates, outside the simulator:

  • Size. A mirror cannot be brighter than the Sun it reflects. To deliver 1.17 times the Sun's light, the L5 array has to fill at least 1.17 times the Sun's apparent area in Mars's sky. L5 is as far from Mars as the Sun is, so the array must be roughly as large as the Sun itself: at least 1.5 million km across, a reflecting area of about 2 × 1012 km² tilted 30° to the sunlight, and more to shape the beam. L4, which concentrates its light on the caps, is of the same order.
  • Mass. Reflective film weighing a few grams per square metre adds up to about 1016 kg, the material of an asteroid 20–30 km across.
  • Aiming. From 228 million km away, even a perfect mirror spreads the Sun's image into a patch of light about 1.4 million km wide, 200 times wider than Mars, so almost all of each facet's light misses. To shape the light, each facet is aimed so the sharp edge of its sun-image falls across Mars, lighting the planet on one side of a line and not the other. Many facets with their edges at different latitudes build up any profile, the lens of L5 or the caps of L4.
  • Light pressure. Sunlight pushes on a mirror. For film of 3 g/m² the push is about 40% of the Sun's pull, so the arrays would sit somewhat off the classical L4 and L5 points and need constant steering, unless the film is heavier: at about 25 g/m² the push drops under 5%.

Part II

How the model works

The simulator computes its world in five stages, each feeding the next. Every model was first built and tested on Earth, with Earth's radius, spin, gravity, air and terrain, and then run with Mars's numbers. This part goes through the stages in order, with their equations and constants.

14Orbit and time

Mars follows a Keplerian orbit: eccentricity e = 0.0934, semi-major axis a = 1.5237 AU, perihelion at Ls 250.9°, tilt 25.19°. A year is 686.98 Earth days, or 668.6 sols of 88,775 s (24 h 39 m 35 s). The simulator's clocks use Mars hours, a 24th of a sol (1.0275 Earth hours).

For a time t in sols the position comes from Kepler's equation, solved by Newton's method:

M = M₀ + 2π t / 668.6
E − e sin E = M
ν = 2 atan[√((1 + e)/(1 − e)) tan(E/2)]
Ls = ν + 250.9°, r = a (1 − e cos E)

The Sun's declination, the latitude where it stands overhead at noon, is δ = asin(sin 25.19° · sin Ls). Its light above the air is 1,361 W/m² divided by r² (r in AU): 713 W/m² at perihelion, 490 at aphelion.

  • Two ways of counting. The model's clock, and the charts, count sols from the northern winter solstice (Ls 270°). The calendar counts from New Year at Ls 0°, sol 1 being Novary 1; the winter solstice then falls on Fervidor 4, sol 515.
  • Clock time. Times of day are local true solar time: 12:00 is when the Sun crosses the meridian. Because of the eccentric orbit, a sundial and a steady clock disagree by up to −51 to +40 minutes over the year. The Today button finds today's Ls with the formula of Allison & McEwen (2000).
  • The circular reference. Each chart can also show a planet on a circular orbit with the same tilt and the same starting solstice: its distance stays at 1.5237 AU and Ls advances at a steady rate, so its four seasons last 167.1 sols each, against 193, 179, 143 and 154 for the real ones.

15Sunlight and daylight

Insolation is measured at the top of the air, on level ground. For one source with declination δ, a place at latitude φ gets on average over the sol

Q = (S / π) · (H₀ sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ sin H₀)
cos H₀ = −tan φ tan δ

where S is the source's light at Mars and H₀ the hour angle of sunset (0 in polar night, π in polar day). Each mirror array is treated as a second source of the same kind:

  • Its own declination. Seen from Mars, L4 stands 60° behind the Sun along the ecliptic and L5 60° ahead, so their declinations are asin(sin 25.19° · sin(Ls ∓ 60°)). In northern spring, for instance, L4 lights the planet as the Sun would in the southern summer and L5 as in the northern summer.
  • Its own hour. Each crosses the meridian about 4 Mars hours before (L4) or after (L5) the Sun, a little more or less with the season, because the ecliptic is tilted against the equator.
  • Its own strength and shape. S is the stated multiple of sunlight, times the shaping profile of section 16.

Daylight runs from the moment the first sun's centre rises above a flat horizon to the moment the last one sets, merging the arcs of all the suns whose light reaches the place: L4 adds no daylight between its two edges. Refraction and terrain are ignored. The peak is the brightest moment of the sol, adding all the suns. The seasonal ratio divides each sol's light by the dimmest sol of the year at the same latitude.

Earth comparison lines on the charts are typical Earth values for the same measure: daily means of 100 and 500 W/m² for mid-latitude winter and summer and 250 and 450 in the tropics; noon peaks of 350 and 1,300 W/m² for mid-latitude winter and summer, 700 for tropical winter and 1,000 at the equator.

16Shaping the mirror light

The lens of L5

Across the disc of Mars it lights, the array's beam goes as

f(a) = 1 − k cos 2(a − p)
f(a) = 1 + k once |a − p| passes 90°

where a is the angle from the lit disc's east–west centre line. On the ground that centre line is a great circle through the point under the array, tilted with the array's declination, so a equals the real latitude when the array is over the equator. The profile is divided by its average over the disc, N(k, p) = ∫ f(a) cos²a da / (π/2) over −90° to 90° (computed exactly), so the array always delivers its stated average. Without a phase the average is 1 − k/2, and k = 1 would leave the centre line dark with the north and south edges at 4 times the average.

The phase and the depth can follow the seasons:

p(Ls) = bias + A sin(Ls − shift) + A₂ sin 2(Ls − s₂)
k(Ls) = k + a sin(Ls − s)

The three-sun defaults are bias 7°, A = 27.5°, shift 8°, A₂ = 12°, s₂ = 54°, k = 0.85 with no swing; the two-sun defaults are k = 1 swinging by ±0.2, with the dimmest line swinging ±27.5° (plus ±6.5° twice a year) around 11.5°N.

The caps of L4

  • Where. Only poleward of a north edge and a south edge, neither ever closer to the equator than 45°. At each edge the light rises from zero over a soft ramp (a smoothstep 9° wide).
  • Leaning. Inside each cap the light follows the array's own unshaped pattern, tilted toward the edge or the pole by the pole lean g: at g = −0.9 the pole gets a tenth of what the edge gets.
  • Through the year. The two edges, the share sent north and the strength are set at Ls 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° and joined by a smooth periodic curve (a Catmull–Rom spline).
  • Limits. Each cap receives its share of the array's light, but no latitude more than 10 times what the array would give it unshaped; what one cap cannot take goes to the other.

17Wavelengths and the UV index

Of sunlight above the air, 8.0% lies below 400 nm (ultraviolet: 0.5% UV-C, 1.3% UV-B, 6.3% UV-A), 39.0% between 400 and 700 nm (visible) and 53.0% above 700 nm, from the ASTM E-490/G-173 extraterrestrial spectrum. UV-free mirrors reflect none of the first part and 1.087 times as much of the rest, so 42.4% of their light is visible.

The UV index is the clear-sky value at the ground, from Madronich's formula for Earth's air at 1 AU:

UVI = 12.5 · μ2.42 · (Ω / 300 DU)−1.23
· (1 / r²) · m−0.6

with μ the cosine of the Sun's zenith angle and Ω the ozone column, taken as Earth's 300 DU. The last two factors are added for Mars: the Sun's distance, and m, the air column relative to Earth's at sea level. The exponent −0.6 reproduces the 6–8% rise of the UV index per km of height measured on Earth. At 1.05 atm, m = 2.77, which alone lowers the index to 0.54 times; on the map it rises about 7% per Earth-equivalent km. Clouds are left out.

The climate effect of UV-free mirrors comes from a simple column model: Earth's ozone layer, Rayleigh scattering for the air column, grey clouds and ground, and a colder stratosphere sending part of its lost heating less back down. It puts the warming at that of 2.0% more mirror light at 1.05 atm (uncertain by about ±0.65%), and the temperature and rain models take it as that.

18Temperature: the energy balance

The temperatures come from a seasonal energy-balance model of the kind climate scientists use for first estimates (Budyko–Sellers type, after North & Coakley 1979). In its simplest form each 2° latitude band has a land box and a sea box, each obeying

C · dT/dt = (1 − α) Q − (A + B T)
+ transport + land–sea exchange
  • Sunlight absorbed, (1 − α) Q, with Earth's cloud albedo but no snow or sea ice: a top-of-atmosphere albedo of 0.24–0.27 in the tropics, rising only to about 0.44 toward the poles, like Earth's ice-free Southern Ocean (Earth's poles reach 0.65).
  • Heat radiated to space, A + B T = 203.3 + 2.09 · T W/m² with T in °C, Earth's fit to satellite measurements, which includes Earth's greenhouse effect.
  • Heat stored, C: on Earth 1.0 × 107 J/m²/K for land and air and 1.0 × 108 for a 24 m ocean mixed layer.
  • Heat moved: 2.0 W/m²/K between land and sea, and poleward by diffusion at 0.649 W/m²/K.

The heat capacities and exchanges were fitted to Earth's seasons with Earth's real albedo, ice included. On Earth's orbit the model gives a 15 °C global mean, 29 °C at the equator and −7 / 23 °C (January / July) on land at 45°N.

For Mars three things change:

  • Land heat capacity is soil (3.0 × 106 J/m²/K on Earth, × 1.37 for the deeper layer that Mars's longer seasons reach) plus the air that takes part in the seasons (7.0 × 106 for Earth's air, scaled with the air above, so less over high ground). The ocean box gains the extra air too.
  • Heat transport (diffusion, land–sea exchange and winds) scales with pressure × (Earth's spin rate / Mars's)², as in Williams & Kasting (1997), which comes to about Earth's at 1 atm.
  • The greenhouse is held at Earth's, assuming an air composition tuned to Earth-like warming.

On Earth the formulas give back the calibrated model exactly. The year is periodic, so it is solved harmonic by harmonic (the yearly mean plus five harmonics), exact to well under 0.1 K. The continental, mixed and maritime settings make land 85%, 50% or 15% of every band; the default dynamic setting uses the map's own land and water instead (next section).

19The dynamic grid

The dynamic setting runs the same energy balance on a 4° × 6° latitude–longitude grid with the map's own ocean, seas and lakes:

  • Each cell's heat capacity mixes its water and land shares.
  • Heat diffuses north–south as in the zonal model, and along latitude circles at a tenth of that.
  • Earth-like prevailing low-level winds carry heat east or west: trade winds to 30°, westerlies from 30° to 60°, weak polar easterlies.

The wind strength and the east–west mixing were fitted on Earth's land and sea to the January–July temperature range of 23 mid-latitude cities: within 4.3 K RMS (r = 0.89), against 6.8 K with diffusion alone. Cities next to Earth's winter sea ice, like Yakutsk and Fairbanks, are far more extreme than the model, which has no ice. Ocean currents are not modelled as such: the poleward heat transport fitted on Earth includes the oceans' share on average, spread smoothly over land and sea, without gyres, warm boundary currents, upwelling or deep circulation.

20Height and water temperatures

  • Land. Each map pixel takes its cell's sea-level temperature and cools it by the lapse rate times its height above sea level, in Earth-equivalent km (section 09).
  • Earth-equivalent km multiply Mars heights by gMars/gEarth = 0.38. With the same air, pressure falls that much more slowly with height on Mars, so Olympus Mons, about 24 km above the sea, thins the air like a 9.2 km climb on Earth.
  • Water. The ocean takes its cell's sea-level temperature. A lake's is moved by the lapse rate to the lake's own surface height, so it is cooler than the open ocean by about 2.3 K for each km its surface stands above sea level: Hellas at its brim, 4.4 km up, by about 10 K. The grid has one value per cell for land and water together, so coastal water carries some of the land's seasons.
  • Extremes. The 10th warmest and 10th coldest sol are each place's temperature on the 10th warmest (coldest) sol of its year: a hot or cold spell that comes round every year rather than a one-off extreme.
  • Temperature by latitude. The panel reads each 2° band's land cell by cell, each patch cooled by its own height, and leaves out the ocean, the lakes and ground above a cut-off (3 Earth-equivalent km by default). Summaries weight each band by its counted land.

21Day and night

The energy balance gives each sol's average. The rise to the afternoon high and the fall to the dawn low come from a linear balance for the ground and the air near it:

C · dT′/dt = η F(t) − λ T′

driven by the light of all the suns over the sol, F(t), and solved harmonic by harmonic over the sol. It was fitted to typical daily ranges on Earth's land: desert summer 16 K, continental mid-latitude summer 13 K and winter 9 K, humid tropics 9.5 K, maritime summer 8 K and winter 5.5 K, with the high near 14:30. The fit gives η = 0.59 of the light warming the ground, C = 5.2 × 105 J/m²/K (the soil and the air mixed by day), λ = 49 W/m²/K in dry country and 115 in moist country, and 6.0 K from passing weather added in quadrature, all within about 1 K of those ranges.

  • For Mars, the sol is 2.7% longer, and the range scales with the air column overhead as m−0.4. That exponent gives both the wider ranges of Earth's high plateaus and the 50–70 K range of today's thin Martian air. At 1.05 atm the range is 0.67 times Earth's for the same sunlight and humidity.
  • Moist means near water (the share of ocean, seas and lakes in a 4° × 6° cell and its neighbours, full at 35%) or, once the rain is known, rain close to the potential evaporation (full at P/Ep = 1, none below 0.3). Water surfaces swing 6% as much as moist land.
  • Three suns spread the light over a longer day, since the morning sun rises before the Sun and the evening sun sets after it, so the range is mostly 4–7 K at the defaults.

22Winds and rain

Rain comes from a seasonal moisture budget on a 2° grid: water vapour is evaporated, carried by the winds, held back by terrain and rained out. Each of 12 slices of the year is solved as a steady state, twice: the second time with the soil water the first pass leaves. It runs in the background, in about 15 seconds; the default skies' rain is stored in the page.

Water vapour

Each column holds water vapour up to a saturation amount of ρv,sat · RvT² / (LΓ) per metre, which is about 2.6 times Earth's at the same temperature because Mars's air column is deeper. The vapour is carried by low-level, moisture-weighted winds and mixed by eddies.

Winds

  • The Hadley cells. Near the surface the air flows toward the latitudes of highest moist static energy: warm, moist air means low surface pressure, and friction and the Coriolis force turn the flow, as in Lindzen and Nigam's tropical boundary-layer model applied to the zonal mean. So the rising branch sits wherever that energy peaks (on Earth over the warmest seas rather than the hottest deserts), even near a pole, and the cells cross the equator into the summer hemisphere. Flow toward the equator turns into trade winds, flow toward a pole into westerlies.
  • Their reach. Each cell extends as far as the eddy-regime scaling allows, with the thermal Rossby number to the ¼ power: about 1.4 times Earth's width on Mars.
  • Beyond them come eddy-driven westerlies, the Ferrel cell and stronger eddy mixing, growing with the temperature gradient; monsoon flow follows the land–sea temperature contrasts.

Terrain

Across each step in surface height, only the vapour above the higher ground is exchanged, less the height the wind can lift air by (wind speed ÷ buoyancy frequency, after Sheppard). So moist low air rains out on the windward side and high plateaus stay dry.

When it rains

Rain falls once the column passes 50% of saturation, on a 4.7-day timescale; more where the low-level flow converges (rising air) and much less where it diverges (sinking air, as over Earth's subtropical deserts). Storm tracks add rain where it cools poleward, and air blown up slopes condenses.

Evaporation

The ocean and lakes evaporate by a bulk formula capped by the year's mean net radiation (water stores heat), and not at all while frozen. Land evaporates at a Priestley–Taylor rate, its yearly share set by the Budyko curve in Fu's form (w = 2.6, the global calibration on river runoff of Zhang et al. 2004) from the year's rain and demand. A chosen date interpolates between the slices.

23Tested on Earth

The rain model was run with Earth's radius, rotation, gravity and topography (ETOPO, 20′). Its constants were fitted with Earth's observed temperatures, winds, vertical motion and sea temperatures (NCEP and OISST long-term means) against observed rain (GPCP) and the natural sizes of Earth's closed-basin lakes; the circulation part was fitted separately to the observed low-level winds and their convergence.

  • With observed temperatures and winds, rain over land correlates 0.85 with the observed, typically within a factor of 1.6, and the global total is 1,020 mm a year against 983.
  • Run as it runs for Mars, from the energy-balance temperatures and its own winds, it does worse: correlation 0.63, typically within a factor of 2.1, 870 mm a year globally.

24Lakes

  • Finding the basins. A priority flood from the ocean (8-connected, wrapping round in longitude) finds every closed depression, the height of its brim and the drainage tree that links it to the next basin or the sea. A basin with several pits under one rim is one lake.
  • Hellas and Argyre lie below sea level but are walled off, so by default they are closed seas with their own surfaces.
  • The balance. Each lake rises until evaporation from its surface equals what comes in: the runoff from its catchment (rain minus land evaporation), the rain on it and the overflow from the basins upstream, all yearly means from the rain model. Basins are solved from the top of each drainage tree down, so each knows its upstream overflow before its own level is found.
  • Spilling. A basin that fills spills through its lowest outlet into the next basin or the sea.
  • Feedback. The lakes change the rain and the temperatures in turn, so after a change of sky the balance is repeated, up to three times, until the lake area settles. For the default three-sun and two-sun skies the result is stored.

With the default sky the balance fills Hellas and Argyre to their brims, from a dry start as well. At 35–55°S open water gets about as much rain as it evaporates, as Earth's oceans do at those latitudes. Same fill everywhere replaces the balance with one share of each basin's depth, and sets Hellas and Argyre by hand.

25Rivers

The yearly runoff from the rain model (rain minus land evaporation; on lakes, rain minus lake evaporation) is routed downhill along the steepest descent. Each lake gathers its inflow and passes water on through its outlet only if it is full; past the outlet the water follows the first ground that drains away from the lake.

Flows above about 25 m³/s, a sizeable river, are drawn, far wider than real so they show at the scale of a planet. Where a river runs into a lake that spills, it carries on across the lake as a straight line from where it enters to the spill point and then along the overflow, so a chain of lakes reads as one river. That is drawn for the main inflows (at least 30% of what leaves the lake) where the line stays on the water, and not across Hellas, Argyre or the largest lakes; smaller streams end at the shore.

26Finding the seasons

The five seasons of section 11 come from a fit, not a guess:

  • What is compared. For every land cell of the 4° × 6° grid between 45°S and 45°N, its temperature on each sol of the model's year minus its yearly mean, weighted by its land area. For the version with rain, each 2° land cell's rain through the year is added the same way, scaled so the rain carries as much total variation as the temperature.
  • The score. A set of seasons replaces each sol's pattern with the average pattern of its season. The score is the share of all the sol-to-sol variation that those averages reproduce (the R² of the fit): 100% would mean every sol of a season looks the same.
  • The search. For each number of equal seasons, every possible start sol is tried and the best kept.

27The map, the globe, lines and colours

  • Relief. Mars Global Surveyor's MOLA gridded topography (MEGDR) at 4 pixels per degree, about 15 km at the equator, stored to the nearest 5 m against the MOLA areoid, in an equirectangular projection that wraps round east–west.
  • Sea level is set by the share of the surface (by area) that lies below it: at 26% it stands 3.12 km below the datum.
  • The globe wraps the same map, with its layers, lines, rivers, grid and sites, on a sphere (WebGL). Its relief is the height of the ground or, where there is water, of the flat water surface, exaggerated ten times by default: at true scale Olympus Mons rises only 0.7% of Mars's radius.

Colour scales

  • Temperature is pinned to fixed temperatures from −60 to 60 °C and spends most of its colour on 0–35 °C, where people live: dark capri just above 0 °C, bright green at 10 °C, yellow at 20, orange at 30 and pure red at 35, then darker, ashier reds to 60. At 0 °C the colour flips to a light azure, which turns bluer and darker as it gets colder, so freezing stands out at a glance. The legend gives 0–35 °C two-thirds of its width.
  • Rain doubles with each colour: 300 mm a year yellow, 600 mm green, 1.2 m cyan, 2.4 m purple, 4.8 m magenta.
  • Insolation uses a fixed scale by default, or stretches between the lowest and highest values on land.

Lines

  • Isotherms every 1–30 °C, counted from 0 °C, which is drawn thick.
  • Rain lines every 10 mm to 1 m a year up to the first line at or above 600 mm, then twice that gap up to the first line at or above 2.4 m, then four times it: every 100 mm, for instance, becomes every 200 mm from 600 mm and every 400 mm from 2.4 m. The 300 mm line, roughly the edge of dry country, is always drawn thick.
  • Insolation lines every 100 W/m² (thick at 100 and 500); visible light every 50 W/m²; the peak UV index every whole number, thick at the WHO category boundaries 3, 6, 8 and 11.
  • Height and depth lines every 0.1–5 km in the chosen units, every fifth thicker.
  • Thickness. One slider scales all of these lines (×0.25 to ×3), another the coastlines drawn when water is coloured too (off to ×3). Solid colour between lines fills each band between two neighbouring lines with the colour of its middle value.
  • While the year plays or a dial is dragged, the map shows a half-resolution preview and its lines are traced from it about eight times a second; labels return once it settles.

28What is left out

  • Ocean currents. The heat the oceans carry poleward is included on average, spread evenly over land and sea, but there are no gyres, warm boundary currents like the Gulf Stream, upwelling or deep circulation.
  • Snow, sea ice and changing clouds, and the feedbacks they bring.
  • Mountains blocking heat transport. Rain does see terrain; temperatures only through height.
  • Dust, soils and vegetation.
  • Slopes and the atmosphere in the insolation layer, which shows flat ground at the top of the air.
  • Time. How the planet got here, and how long its air and water would last, are outside the model.

Part III

Martian quirks

Mars looks Earth-like at first glance: a day of almost 24 hours and a similar tilt. The differences behind that resemblance shape every part of the project.

Orbit and calendar

490–713 W/m²

A lopsided orbit

Mars's distance from the Sun runs from 1.38 to 1.67 AU, so its sunlight swings by 45% over the year; Earth's swings by 7%. Because the mirrors share Mars's distance from the Sun, their light swings with it.

193 · 179 · 143 · 154 sols

Unequal seasons

Northern spring, summer, autumn and winter. Perihelion comes at Ls 251°, just before the northern winter solstice, so the southern summer is short and hot and the southern winter long and cold. The three-sun sky is built around it: L4 switches off at perihelion and sends its light south through the long southern winter. Its climate follows five seasons of its own instead.

Ls 0°–360°

A calendar by angle

Mars's seasons are counted by the solar longitude, Ls: the angle Mars has moved round the Sun since the northern spring equinox. Ls 90° is the northern summer solstice, 180° the autumn equinox, 270° the winter solstice. Equal steps of Ls take unequal time.

24 h 39 m 35 s

A slightly longer day

A sol is 2.7% longer than an Earth day. The simulator's clocks use Mars hours, a 24th of a sol (1.0275 Earth hours), on local solar time, so 12:00 is when the Sun crosses the meridian.

668.6 sols

A long year

A Martian year lasts 687 Earth days, 1.88 Earth years. Each season lasts about twice as long as on Earth, giving the ground more time to heat up and cool down, and the seasonal heat reaches deeper into the soil.

−51 to +40 min

Sundials and clocks disagree

Because of the eccentric orbit, a Martian sundial runs up to about 50 minutes ahead of or behind a steady clock over the year. On Earth the difference is at most 16 minutes.

The sky

0.35°

A smaller, fainter Sun

From Mars the Sun is about two-thirds as wide as from Earth (0.53°) and gives 43% of the light.

05:40 → 23:42

Three suns, long days

The morning sun at L4 rises 4 Mars hours before the Sun and the evening sun at L5 sets 4 Mars hours after it. At 45°N in northern spring there is light from 05:40 to 23:42. Each array looks at least as wide as the Sun, since it can be no brighter. From the populated band L4 would look dark, its light aimed at the caps.

2 moons

A moon that rises in the west

Phobos circles Mars faster than Mars turns, so it rises in the west and sets in the east, twice a sol. Deimos rises in the east and takes about two and a half sols to set. Neither is in the simulator.

5261 Eureka

Neighbours at L5

Mars's Lagrange points already hold a few Trojan asteroids, most of them at L5, the second sun's home.

25.19°

A wandering tilt

Mars's tilt is close to Earth's 23.44° today, but with no large moon to steady it, it has swung between about 15° and 45° over the last 10 million years. The sky is designed for today's tilt.

Gravity and air

2.77× the air

Heavy air columns

Mars's gravity is 0.38 g, so 1.05 atm needs 2.77 times Earth's air above each square metre. Pressure falls 2.6 times more slowly with height: at the same temperature the air thins by a factor e every 22 km, against 8.4 km on Earth.

× 0.38

Earth-equivalent heights

Multiplying Mars heights by 0.38 gives the Earth climb with the same drop in pressure. Olympus Mons, 24 km above the sea, thins the air like a 9.2 km climb on Earth, and the highlands at 3.7 km keep 0.89 atm.

2.6× the vapour

More water in the air

The deeper air column holds 2.6 times as much water vapour as Earth's at the same temperature, which matters for how much rain a storm can carry.

0.67× the swing

Gentler days and nights

More air overhead damps the daily cycle: for the same sunlight and humidity, the range between day and night is two-thirds of Earth's. Today's thin Martian air swings by 50–70 K.

UV index 2.5–3.5

A weak sunburn

The fainter Sun, the heavy air column and UV-free mirrors keep the tropical noon UV index at sea level a quarter of Earth's. Sunscreen is optional; vitamin D comes slowly.

Land and water

30 km of relief

Highest and lowest

From the floor of Hellas, about 8 km below the datum, to the top of Olympus Mons, about 21 km above it, Mars's relief spans almost 30 km. Earth's, from the Mariana Trench to Everest, spans 20.

North low, south high

A planet of two halves

The northern third of Mars lies several kilometres lower than the cratered southern highlands. That is why the ocean fills the north and the south is a high continent dotted with lakes.

+4.4 km

Seas above the sea

Full Hellas stands 4.4 km above the ocean and is 9.3 km deep, a sea perched on the highlands, like the Caspian lifted onto a plateau. Its water is about 10 K cooler than the ocean at the same latitude.

14% lakes

A planet of lakes

Besides Hellas and Argyre, crater lakes cover about 14% of Mars. Thousands of old impact craters become lakes, many spilling into the next.

Weather and climate

1.4× wider

Wider tropics

The tropical circulation cells reach about 1.4 times as far from the equator as Earth's, by the scaling for how far they can extend, which depends on the planet's size, spin and temperature contrasts.

26 °C at 80°N

Warmest in the far north

With three suns the land averages about 21–22 °C over the year (at sea level) from 60°S to 45°N, and the warmest land is the far north, around 26 °C beyond 60°N, where L4's light lands. The tropics are not the warmest latitudes, so they get their rain from circulation cells crossing the equator rather than from an Earth-like tropical rain belt.

No ice caps

Temperate poles

With L4 lighting the caps, land north of 45°N never has a sol averaging below −20 °C and land south of 45°S has about one a year. Winter snow could still fall in places, but away from the highest volcano summits nowhere is cold enough for an ice cap. The model itself has no ice.

Cold spring

The light comes first

The climate lags the light by about a season. The populated band gets the least light in Autumn but stays mild on stored heat, and is coldest in Spring, when the light is already rising.

Glossary

Ls, solar longitude
The angle Mars has moved round the Sun since the northern spring equinox: 0° spring equinox, 90° summer solstice, 180° autumn equinox, 270° winter solstice (northern seasons).
Sol
A Martian solar day, 24 h 39 m 35 s. A Mars hour is a 24th of it.
Perihelion, aphelion
The closest (1.38 AU) and farthest (1.67 AU) points of Mars's orbit from the Sun, at Ls 251° and 71°.
L4, L5
Lagrange points of the Sun–Mars pair, on Mars's orbit 60° ahead of and behind the planet, where the mirror arrays sit.
Populated band
Land between 45°S and 45°N, where the comfort targets apply.
Insolation
Sunlight arriving on level ground at the top of the air, in W/m², as a daily mean or a peak.
MOLA datum
Mars's reference level for heights, from the Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter. The default sea stands 3.12 km below it.
Earth-equivalent km
Mars heights × 0.38: the climb on Earth that thins the air as much.
Lapse rate
How fast the air cools with height; 6 K per Earth-equivalent km here.
Albedo
The share of sunlight reflected back to space.
Energy balance model
A climate model that balances absorbed sunlight against heat radiated to space, stored and carried away.
Moist static energy
The heat, height and water vapour of air together; near-surface air flows toward where it peaks.
Budyko curve
The relation between rain, evaporative demand and how much of the rain the land evaporates over a year.
Festival sol
A calendar day outside the weeks: Perihelia, Finalia and, in leap years, Finalia II.
