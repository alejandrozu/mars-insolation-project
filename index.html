Model notes

How each number is computed, in brief. The guide explains every choice and the model in full.
  • Keplerian orbit with e = 0.0934 and a = 1.5237 AU. Perihelion falls at Ls 250.9°, just before the northern winter solstice, so northern autumn and winter are the short seasons and the southern summer gets the strongest sunlight.
  • Five seasons and the calendar (the date dials): with three suns the astronomical seasons do not follow the weather, because the mirrors cancel most of the tilt's seasons and leave the orbit's, which the whole planet shares: both halves of the populated band warm toward perihelion and cool after aphelion. Splitting the model's year into seasons that best group similar sols (land temperature, and rain, between 45°S and 45°N) gives five, named after the five traditional Spanish seasons (Estia is the late, dry summer). The calendar starts the year at Ls 0°, as Mars years are counted, and gives each season four months of 6-sol weeks: 36-sol months of six weeks and 30-sol months of five. Autumn (Ls 0–65°: Novary, Pluviary, Nimbary, Lenary; mild, the north's wet season), Winter (65–129°: Aphelose, Umbrose, Frigose, Brumose; from aphelion, on Aphelose 14: cool and quiet, deep winter in the far south, the southern band at its driest) and Spring (129–206°: Vertal, Pruinal, Equinal, Vernal; the band's coldest weeks, about 17 °C around Ls 168°, then the north warms fast) last 138 sols. Summer (206–287°: Lucidor, Calidor, Heliodor, Fervidor; perihelion on Heliodor 5: the band at its warmest, monsoon rain in the south) and Estia (287–360°: Torrival, Ardival, Mitival, Finival; cooling from the peak, the north at its driest, the far south at its hottest) last 127, because the climate changes fast near perihelion. Two festival sols sit outside the weeks, so every month starts on the first day of a week: Perihelia, the 31st of Calidor, five sols before perihelion, and Finalia, the 31st of Finival, the last sol of the year, with Finalia II after it in leap years. A year has 668 sols, or 669 in leap years: odd years and years divisible by 10, except centuries not divisible by 500 (668.592 sols on average; the model year is 668.60). The names are Greco-Latin: Novary from novus (new), Pluviary from pluvia (rain), Nimbary from nimbus (rain cloud), Lenary from lenis (mild), Aphelose from aphelion, Umbrose from umbra (shade), Frigose from frigus (cold), Brumose from bruma (midwinter), Vertal from vertere (to turn), Pruinal from pruina (hoarfrost), Equinal from the equinox it holds, Vernal from ver (spring), Lucidor from lux (light), Calidor from calidus (warm), Heliodor from helios (the Sun, of perihelion), Fervidor from fervidus (glowing), Torrival from torrere (to parch), Ardival from ardere (to burn), Mitival from mitis (softening) and Finival from finis (end). The seasons capture about 80% of the band's seasonal temperature changes, against 63% for the four astronomical quarters. The date dials show the months on their outer ring and the seasons inside it, and can give the date as month and day, as the sol of the year (from 1 at Ls 0°) or as Ls; the charts still count sols from the northern winter solstice.
  • One year is 686.98 Earth days, or 668.6 sols. A sol lasts 24 h 39 m 35 s, so one Mars hour (1/24 sol) is 1.0275 Earth hours.
  • Daylight runs from the moment the Sun's centre rises above a flat horizon to the moment it sets. Refraction in the thin atmosphere and local terrain are ignored.
  • Insolation is measured at the top of the atmosphere on level ground: 1361 W/m² at 1 AU, scaled by distance squared (713 W/m² at perihelion, 490 W/m² at aphelion). Dust can remove a large share of this before it reaches the surface.
  • Light by wavelength (All light, Visible and UV index on the insolation and ratio charts and the map's insolation layer): of sunlight above the atmosphere, 8.0% lies below 400 nm (ultraviolet: 0.5% UV-C, 1.3% UV-B, 6.3% UV-A), 39.0% between 400 and 700 nm (visible) and 53.0% above 700 nm (the ASTM E-490/G-173 extraterrestrial spectrum). Visible light is shown in W/m² at the top of the atmosphere, like the total. The UV index is the clear-sky value at the ground: 12.5 μ2.42 (Ω/300 DU)−1.23 for Earth's air at 1 AU (Madronich 2007, with μ the cosine of the Sun's zenith angle and Ω the ozone column). Here it assumes an Earth-like ozone layer (300 DU), scales sunlight with the Sun's distance, and counts the air overhead as m−0.6, with m the air column relative to Earth's at sea level, which reproduces the 6–8% rise per km of height measured on Earth. Under Mars gravity 1.05 atm is 2.77× Earth's air, which alone lowers the index to 0.54×; on the map it rises about 7% per Earth-equivalent km of height. With sunlight at 36–52% of Earth's and that much air, the Sun's noon peak at sea level in the tropics is about 2.5–3.5 (aphelion to perihelion), against 10–12 in Earth's tropics: “low” to “moderate” on the WHO scale. Clouds are left out.
  • UV-free mirrors (on by default; the switch in the sky bar): the arrays reflect nothing below 400 nm and 1.087× as much of the rest, so each still delivers its stated total and 42.4% of its light is visible (39.0% of sunlight is). Only the natural Sun then sends ultraviolet. That changes the climate a little. Ozone in the stratosphere would have absorbed the mirrors' UV-B and UV-C, about 2% of their light, and the air scatters near-UV light back to space more than visible light. The replacement visible and infrared light mostly reaches the lower air and the ground instead. A simple column model (Earth's ozone layer, Rayleigh scattering for the air column, grey clouds and ground, with the stratosphere, now colder, sending half of its lost heating less back down) puts the warming at that of 2.0% more mirror light at 1.05 atm (1.5% with Earth's air column), uncertain by about ±0.65%, and the temperature and rain models take it as that. At the three-sun defaults of the time (before the CO₂ setting) land became about 1.3 °C warmer: sols below 0 °C between 45°S and 45°N go from about 14 to 8 a year (above 30 °C from fewer than 1 to 2), land north of 45° passes 30 °C on about 25 sols instead of 18, and rain over land rises 9%; Hellas and Argyre stay full. With two suns land is also about 1.3 °C warmer (sols below 0 °C in the populated band 40 → 29, above 30 °C 1 → 3) and rain over land rises about 8% with the lakes held as they are. Rebalancing them can undo that: with a 30% sea the warmer lakes evaporated more, Argyre stopped filling, and the land ended up slightly drier. The stratosphere, warmed only by the Sun's UV, would be colder than Earth's. The mirrors' visible light also breaks up ozone (the Chappuis band) without the UV-C that makes it, so the ozone layer could be thinner than assumed, raising the UV index a little. Photosynthesis uses 400–700 nm, so plants lose nothing they need; vitamin D in skin needs UV-B, which the Sun alone would supply at roughly a quarter of Earth's tropical UV index.
  • The circular orbit (shown as a reference or on its own, per chart) keeps the same tilt and the same starting solstice, fixes the distance at 1.5237 AU and advances Ls at a constant rate, so all four seasons last 167.1 sols.
  • Two and three suns: mirror arrays at the Sun–Mars L4 and L5 points. They sit at Mars's distance from the Sun, so seen from Mars they shine from the orbital plane 60° west (L4) or east (L5) of the Sun, and their light is a fixed multiple of sunlight at Mars's current distance. Daylight counts any sun above the horizon whose light reaches the place: the supplemental sun of three suns lights only the outer latitudes, so it adds no daylight in the band between its edges; the peak is the brightest moment of the sol. 1.33× brings the average flux to about Earth's. Three suns sets each array's strength and shaping separately. L5, the second sun, keeps the lens profile and moderates the middle latitudes. L4, the supplemental sun, can instead light only the outer latitudes: poleward of a north edge and a south edge that never come closer to the equator than 45°, rising from zero at the edge over a soft ramp (a smoothstep a few degrees wide) and leaning toward or away from the pole. The two edges, the share of its light sent north and its strength (a share of the set value) are given at Ls 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° and joined by a smooth periodic curve (Catmull-Rom). Inside each cap the light follows what the array would give unshaped, and no latitude gets more than 10× that; what one cap cannot take goes to the other. The populated middle band then sees only the Sun and L5, so nights remain. The default (now L5 1.10× Sun; L4 up to 0.51×, 0.32× on average) was chosen in the dynamic setting with the wet-climate defaults (1.05 atm, 5 K per Earth-equivalent km, Hellas, Argyre and the craters holding the lakes their own water balance gives) to keep land between 45°S and 45°N inside 0–30 °C, the ocean north of the equator between 20 and 30 °C (aiming a degree inside that range), and land poleward of 45°, north and south, inside −20 to 30 °C on as many sols as possible, with no light left undelivered; it was then nudged, without adding sols outside those ranges, toward more rain on the southern highlands. That was done with the earlier rain model and mirrors that reflect the whole spectrum. Later its north edge was moved from 45–52°N to 50–57°N (51°, 51°, 57° and 50°N at Ls 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°), and its north share raised (100%, 53%, 100% and 31%) so the northern cap still gets the same light. That cools the band between 45°S and 45°N by about 0.4 °C and the planet by about 0.3 °C, because light sent further poleward meets more cloud and is reflected more. Last, when the lapse rate was raised from 5 to 6 K per Earth-equivalent km, the sky was retuned to cool the band without drying it: L4 sends less of its northern-summer light north (16% instead of 53% at Ls 90°) and runs at 69% instead of 77% at Ls 0°, and L5's dimmest line swings 27.5° instead of 29°, with a 7° bias instead of 5° and a ±12° semiannual swing instead of ±8°, while its k no longer swings with the seasons. At 6 K/km that takes the sols above 30 °C between 45°S and 45°N from about 12 to 2 a year with the sols below 0 °C about the same (8), rain over land from about 620 to 630 mm a year, and the land under 300 mm from about 20% to 16%; the band's land is about 0.8 °C cooler. With the CO₂ setting (default 700 ppm, +9.2 W/m² over the Earth-fitted greenhouse), L5 was dimmed from 1.17× to 1.10× to offset it: CO₂ warms everywhere, and dimming L5, which lights the whole planet, offsets it cleanly, while dimming L4 instead cooled the northern ocean and the far south and dried the land. With the current rain model, the lakes it gives (Hellas and Argyre full), UV-free mirrors, the default 26% sea, 6 K/km and 700 ppm, land between 45°S and 45°N is outside 0–30 °C on about 7 sols a year, 4 of them below 0 °C on the highest ground; land north of 45° passes 30 °C on about 33 sols, land south of 45° is outside −20 to 30 °C on fewer than 1 sol, and the ocean north of the equator stays at 23–26 °C all year. Counterirradiance (two suns): the array shapes its beam like a concave lens, with intensity 1 − k·cos 2a across the disc it lights, where a is the angle from the disc's east–west centre line, i.e. the latitude as seen from the array. On the ground that centre line is a great circle through the point under the array, tilted with its declination, so a equals the real latitude when the array is over the equator. The profile is divided by its average over the disc, 1 − k/2, so the array still delivers 1× or 1.33× on average; k = 1 leaves the centre line dark and makes the north and south edges 4× the average. Phase p moves the dimmest line to a = p, 1 − k·cos 2(a − p), held at its maximum once |a − p| passes 90°, again divided by its disc average; the phase can be fixed or follow the seasons (p × sin Ls), putting the dimmest line in the summer hemisphere.
  • Earth comparison lines are typical Earth values for the same measure: daily mean 100 and 500 W/m² for mid-latitude winter and summer, 250 and 450 for the tropics; noon peak 350 and 1300 for mid-latitude winter and summer, 700 for tropical winter and 1000 at the equator. For visible light the same values × 0.39. For the UV index, Earth's clear-sky values from the same formula (45° and 15° at the solstices, the equator at the equinox): noon peak 1.1 and 10.5 for mid-latitude winter and summer, 6.9 for tropical winter, 12.5 at the equator; averaged over the day 0.2, 3.0 and 1.5.
  • Seasonal ratio divides each sol’s insolation by the dimmest value of the year at the same latitude, for the chosen measure. Earth references: about 5× (daily mean) and 4× (noon peak) at mid-latitudes, about 2× in the tropics.
  • Map: elevation from the Mars Global Surveyor MOLA gridded topography (MEGDR, 4 pixels per degree, about 15 km at the equator; Smith et al. 2003, NASA PDS Geosciences Node), stored to the nearest 5 m relative to the MOLA areoid. The water level is the share of the surface area that lies below sea level; at 26% (the default) the sea stands 3.12 km below the MOLA datum. Only the water joined to the northern ocean stands at that level (23.8% of the surface); Hellas and Argyre lie below it but are enclosed, so by default they are closed seas with their own surfaces, like the Caspian. With "Fill craters and closed basins" on, every closed depression holds a lake with a flat surface. On Water balance (the default) each lake rises until evaporation from its surface balances the runoff from its catchment (rain minus land evaporation), the rain on it and the overflow from basins upstream, all yearly means from the rain model below; a basin that fills spills through its lowest outlet into the next basin or the sea. A basin with several pits under one rim is treated as one lake. The lakes in turn change the rain and the temperatures, so after a change of sky the balance is repeated, up to three times, until the lake area settles; for the default three-sun and two-sun skies it is stored. With the default sky the balance fills Hellas and Argyre to their brims, and from a dry start as well. Both spill: Hellas's surface then stands at +1.27 km on the MOLA datum, about 4.4 km above the ocean, and Argyre's at +0.14 km; together they cover 7.2% of the surface. At 35–55°S open water gets about as much rain as it evaporates, as Earth's oceans do at those latitudes. Here the full Hellas sea evaporates about 0.51 m a year and receives about 0.51 m of rain, so once it holds water it sustains itself. With two suns the summers are hotter and the far south drier, so Hellas shrinks to a lake about 1.6 km deep on its floor, its surface about 6.5 km below the MOLA datum, evaporating about 1.9 m a year with almost no rain. Argyre is only partly full, and water covers 32% of the planet. The margins are small next to the model's errors on Earth, where it fills closed basins too much (see Rain), so read full seas as the wet end. Craters are on average 87% full north of 45°N, 85% between the equator and 45°N, 96% between the equator and 45°S and 94% south of 45°S (by brim area); about 86% of them end up full and spilling. Same fill everywhere instead fills every basin to one share of its depth with the basin fill slider and sets Hellas and Argyre by hand. The default sky was first chosen with an earlier rain model and last retuned with this one. At these defaults the ocean holds a global layer of 229 m of water, Hellas and Argyre 202 m and the crater lakes 45 m: 476 m in all, covering 45% of Mars. Earth-equivalent km multiply Mars heights by gMars/gEarth = 0.38: with the same air, pressure falls that much more slowly with height on Mars, so Olympus Mons (about 24 km above a 26% sea) thins the air like a 9.2 km climb on Earth. The insolation layer uses the same flat-ground, top-of-atmosphere model as the charts, so it varies with latitude only; slopes and the atmosphere are not included.
  • Insolation-based temperature: what an Earth-like planet would reach with this sunlight, from a seasonal energy-balance model calibrated on Earth (Budyko–Sellers type, after North & Coakley 1979). Each 2° latitude band has a land and a sea box that absorb sunlight with Earth's cloud albedo but no snow or sea ice (top-of-atmosphere albedo 0.24–0.27 in the tropics, rising only to about 0.44 toward the poles, like the ice-free Southern Ocean, where Earth reaches 0.65), radiate 203.3 + 2.09·T W/m² (Earth's satellite fit, T in °C, less the CO₂ greenhouse beyond Earth's: see Sea-level pressure and CO₂), store heat (on Earth: land and air 1.0×10⁷ J/m²/K, a 24 m ocean mixed layer 1.0×10⁸), swap heat with each other (2.0 W/m²/K) and pass it poleward by diffusion (0.649 W/m²/K). The heat capacities and exchange were fitted to Earth's seasons (with Earth's real albedo, ice included): on Earth's orbit the model gives a 15 °C global mean, 29 °C at the equator and −7 / 23 °C (January / July) on land at 45°N in the mixed setting. Continental, mixed and maritime set land to 85%, 50% or 15% of each band; Dynamic uses the map’s own land and water instead (below). The map shows the land temperature, cooled with height above sea level at the lapse rate (default 6 K per Earth-equivalent km, 2.3 K per Mars km). That is the middle of what mountain stations on Earth measure for daily mean temperatures (about 4.5 K/km in the Pyrenees, 5.5 on Mount Washington, from 4.4 in winter to 8.2 in summer in the Alps, 6.9 in the Ecuadorian Andes) and of air at 85% humidity cooling along the moist adiabat (5.5–5.7 over the year, 7.3 in the cool season). Earth's standard atmosphere uses 6.5, an average for the free air up to 11 km rather than for the ground. The default used to be 5, which left the high ground about 1 °C warmer per Earth-equivalent km; at 6, land between 45°S and 45°N has about 4 more sols a year below 0 °C, all of them on high ground. Outside Dynamic it ignores Mars's own oceans and lakes as drawn; it always ignores mountains blocking winds, deserts, clouds that change with climate, and ice that grows when it is cold, so read it as how warm the sunlight makes places, not a forecast. Mars's year is 1.9 Earth years long, so its seasons have more time to heat and cool than Earth's.
  • Sea-level pressure (default 1.05 atm, so the highlands, on average 3.7 km above sea level, still have about 0.89 atm): under Mars gravity (3.72 m/s²) 1 atm means 2.64× Earth’s air per m²; Earth’s air mass would give 0.38 atm. Land heat capacity is soil (3.0×10⁶ J/m²/K on Earth, ×1.37 for the deeper seasonal layer of Mars’s longer year) plus the air taking part in the seasons (7.0×10⁶ for Earth’s air, scaled with the air above, so less over high ground in Dynamic); the ocean box gains the extra air too. Heat transport (diffusion, land–sea exchange, winds) scales with pressure × (Earth’s spin rate / Mars’s)², as in Williams & Kasting (1997): about Earth’s at 1 atm. On Earth the formulas give back the calibrated model exactly. CO₂ (default 700 ppm): the outgoing-heat fit is Earth’s of 1985–89, about 350 ppm under Earth’s air column, and Mars’s sea-level air is p0/0.38 times Earth’s per m² (2.77× at 1.05 atm), so the same mixing ratio puts that much more CO₂ overhead. The extra greenhouse, 5.35 ln(column × ppm / 350) W/m² (Myhre et al. 1998), is taken off the fit’s constant: zero at about 126 ppm (the greenhouse every run had before this setting), +6.2 W/m² at 400 ppm, +9.2 at 700 and +11.1 at 1,000. Water vapour and clouds keep Earth’s greenhouse, though the deeper air column would also hold more water vapour. Mars’s own CO₂, mixed into this air, would make about 4,300 ppm (7,700 with the polar ice), above the 5,000 ppm limit for an 8-hour working day once the ice is counted; 700 ppm assumes most of it removed. With L5 dimmed to keep the sea-level average near 22.8 °C (1.17× with Earth’s greenhouse, 1.13× at 400 ppm, 1.10× at 700, 1.08× at 1,000) the climate barely changes with CO₂, and gets a little wetter: land under 300 mm 16%, 13%, 13% and 8%.
  • Dynamic: the same energy balance on a 4° × 6° latitude–longitude grid with the map’s water level and lakes. A cell’s heat capacity mixes its water and land shares; heat diffuses north–south as in the zonal model and along latitude circles at a tenth of that, and Earth-like prevailing low-level winds carry it east or west (trade winds to 30°, westerlies from 30° to 60°, weak polar easterlies). The wind strength and the zonal mixing were fitted on Earth’s land and sea to the January–July range of 23 mid-latitude cities (approximate climate normals): within 4.3 K RMS (r = 0.89), against 6.8 K with diffusion alone. Cities next to Earth’s winter sea ice (Yakutsk, Fairbanks) are far more extreme than the model, which has no ice. The periodic cycle is solved harmonic by harmonic (yearly mean plus five), exact to well under 0.1 K. The temperature-by-latitude panel reads each band’s land cell by cell, each patch cooled with its own height. Ocean currents are not modelled as such: the poleward heat transport fitted on Earth includes the oceans' share on average, but spread smoothly over land and sea, so there are no gyres, warm boundary currents, upwelling or deep circulation, and no sea ice.
  • 10th warmest and 10th coldest sol: each place's temperature on the 10th warmest (coldest) sol of its year in the temperature model, cooled with height: a hot or cold spell that comes round every year rather than a one-off extreme.
  • Daily high and low (Each sol: Average, High, Low; for every temperature view, so also the seasonal swing of the highs or lows and the 10th warmest or coldest high or low). The energy-balance model gives each sol's average. The rise to the afternoon high and the fall to the dawn low come from a linear balance for the ground and the air near it, C dT′/dt = η F(t) − λ T′. It is driven by the light of all the suns over the sol, so mirrors that rise before the Sun or set after it stretch the warm part of the day and narrow the range, and it is solved harmonic by harmonic over the sol. It was fitted to typical ranges on Earth's land: desert summer 16 K, continental mid-latitude summer 13 K and winter 9 K, humid tropics 9.5 K, maritime summer 8 K and winter 5.5 K, with the high near 14:30. That gives η = 0.59 of the light warming the ground, C = 5.2 × 10⁵ J/m²/K (the soil and the air mixed by day), λ = 49 W/m²/K in dry country and 115 W/m²/K in moist country, and 6.0 K from passing weather added in quadrature, all within about 1 K of those values. For Mars, the sol is 2.7% longer, and the range scales with the air column overhead as m−0.4. That exponent gives both the wider ranges of Earth's high plateaus and the 50–70 K range of today's thin Martian air. Under Mars gravity 1.05 atm is 2.77× Earth's air, so the range is 0.67× Earth's for the same sunlight and humidity. Moist means near water (the share of ocean, seas and lakes in each 4° × 6° cell and its neighbours, full at 35%) or, once the rain has been computed, rain close to the potential evaporation (full at P/Ep = 1, none below 0.3). Water surfaces swing 6% as much as moist land. At the defaults the range is mostly 4–7 K with three suns, which spread the light over the sol, and up to about 11 K in the dry interiors with two suns. The latitude panel still counts daily averages.
  • Rain: a seasonal moisture budget on a 2° grid, solved as a steady state for 12 slices of the year, twice (the second time with the soil water the first pass leaves), in the background (about 15 seconds). Column water vapour is carried by low-level, moisture-weighted winds and mixed by eddies. The winds come from the temperature model. In the Hadley regime the low-level air flows toward the latitudes of highest near-surface moist static energy: warm, moist air means low surface pressure, and friction and the Coriolis force turn the flow, as in Lindzen and Nigam's tropical boundary-layer model applied to the zonal mean. So the rising branch sits wherever that energy peaks (on Earth over the warmest seas rather than the hottest deserts), even near a pole, and the cells cross the equator into the summer hemisphere, as climate models of planets with warm poles show. Flow toward the equator turns into trade winds, flow toward a pole into westerlies. Each cell reaches as far as the eddy-regime scaling allows: the thermal Rossby number to the ¼ power, about 1.4× Earth's width on Mars. Beyond it come eddy-driven westerlies, the Ferrel cell and stronger eddy mixing, growing with the size of the temperature gradient, and monsoon flow follows the land–sea temperature contrasts. Terrain holds moisture back: across each step in surface height only the vapour above the higher ground is exchanged, less the height the wind can lift air by (wind speed ÷ buoyancy frequency, after Sheppard), so moist low air rains out on the windward side and high plateaus stay dry. Rain falls once the column passes 50% of saturation, on a 4.7-day timescale, more where the low-level flow converges (rising air) and much less where it diverges (sinking air, as over Earth's subtropical deserts); storm tracks add rain where it cools poleward, and air blown up slopes condenses. The ocean and lakes evaporate by a bulk formula capped by the year's mean net radiation (water stores heat), none while frozen; land at a Priestley–Taylor rate, its yearly share set by the Budyko curve in Fu's form (w = 2.6, the global calibration on river runoff of Zhang et al. 2004) from the year's rain and demand. The saturation column is ρv,sat × RvT²/(LΓ) per metre, about 2.6× Earth's at the same temperature because Mars's air column is deeper. Chosen date interpolates between the slices.
    Tested on Earth. The same code was run with Earth's radius, rotation, gravity and topography (ETOPO, 20′). Its constants were fitted with Earth's observed temperatures, winds, vertical motion and sea temperatures (NCEP and OISST long-term means) against observed rain (GPCP) and the natural sizes of Earth's closed-basin lakes. With that observed forcing, its rain over land correlates 0.85 with the observed, typically within a factor of 1.6, and it gives 1,020 mm a year globally (observed 983). The circulation part was fitted separately to the observed low-level winds and their convergence. Run as it runs here, from the energy-balance temperatures and its own winds, it does worse: correlation 0.63, typically within a factor of 2.1, 870 mm a year globally. Western Europe gets 980 mm (observed about 1,010), the Sahara 100 (100), the Congo 1,480 (1,700), the Amazon 1,670 (2,490) and Tibet 240 (430). But the summer monsoons come out far too weak: India gets 290 mm against 1,120, eastern China 260 against 1,340 and the eastern US 450 against 1,320. Arabia comes out too wet: 380 mm against 120. It also fills Earth's large closed basins too much. The Caspian, Aral, Balkhash and Tarim basins and the Makgadikgadi pans all come out full, whereas in reality the Caspian covers 371 thousand km², the Aral 68 thousand before 1960, and the others are small or dry. Lake Chad comes out about right (28 thousand km², against 20). So read big closed seas as the wet end of what is possible. In neither default sky are the tropics the warmest latitudes for most of the year. With three suns, the light sent to the outer latitudes makes 50–60°N or the south pole the warmest; with two suns, the lens profile heats the summer hemisphere's high latitudes. The air rises there, and the tropics get their rain from cells crossing the equator rather than from an Earth-like rain belt; Earth cannot test that regime directly. Because the monsoons are weak on Earth, rain on Mars's southern highlands, which depends on monsoon air from the northern ocean, is probably underestimated. At the defaults (a 26% sea, UV-free mirrors, 6 K/km), land gets about 650 mm a year on average with three suns (13% of it under 300 mm, 7% under 250 mm, 0.6% under 100 mm) and about 330 mm with two suns (65% under 300 mm, 60% under 250 mm, 33% under 100 mm), at 700 ppm of CO₂. At 5 K/km it was about 670 mm and 250 mm: colder high ground gets less rain, but its lakes evaporate less and grow, and with two suns the extra lake surface more than makes up for it. Two suns leave the northern ocean cool (about 15 °C on average), so it evaporates little, and the southern highlands are a high continent far from it. Searching the two-sun settings for more rain gave the present two-sun default (k swinging by ±0.2 instead of ±0.6, its timing and the semiannual part moved), which cut the land under 100 mm from 43% to 26% (with full-spectrum mirrors). Wetter two-sun skies exist, but only with tens more sols below 0 °C in the populated band and harsher seasons in the far south. Rain lines can be drawn every 10 mm to 1 m a year (on a chosen date, at that day's rate over a year), with the gap doubled past the first line at or above 600 mm and doubled again past the first at or above 2.4 m (every 100 mm, for instance, then every 200 mm from 600 mm and every 400 mm from 2.4 m); the 300 mm line, roughly the edge of dry country, is always drawn thick. Colours double with each step: 300 mm a year is yellow, 600 mm green, 1.2 m cyan, 2.4 m purple and 4.8 m magenta. Read it as the pattern this sunlight would drive, not a forecast: clouds and their feedbacks are left out.
  • Climate zones (Show: Climate zones): every map pixel is classified from twelve months, the rain model's twelve slices of the year (55.7 sols each): their mean temperatures from the dynamic grid, cooled with the pixel's height, and their rain counted as a rate, in mm per 30.4 Earth days (the year's total per Earth year), so thresholds set for Earth keep their meaning. Summer is each place's warmer half-year. Köppen–Geiger follows Peel et al. (2007), colours from Beck et al. (2018): E if the warmest month is under 10 °C; B if the year's rain is under 10 × (2 × mean + 14) mm (+14 or −14 when 70% falls in summer or winter); A if the coldest month is 18 °C or more, C if above 0 °C, D otherwise. With three suns more than half the land is temperate (Cfb 24%, Csa 16%), a quarter arid, 15% cold (the far south) and 3% polar (volcano tops), and very little tropical (1.4%), because most land stands high. The Martian zones: W warm (mean 15 °C or more), M mild (7–15 °C) or C cold (under 7 °C); then r rainy, h humid, d dry or x desert by the year's rain over the rain model's potential evaporation (1, 0.5, 0.25); then e even or u uneven rain (60% or more of it in one half of the year). A leading s marks a seasonal climate, its warmest and coldest months 20 K or more apart (only the mild ones keep e or u); a leading p a subpolar cold one, its warmest month 5–10 °C (pCr, pCh, pCd, dry including desert). P polar where the warmest month stays under 5 °C, only on the Tharsis tops: Pb 0–5 °C, Pf below 0 °C. With three suns 54% of the land is mild, 37% warm, 9% cold (2.9% subpolar) and 0.6% polar; almost a fifth is seasonal (the far south and the flanks of Tharsis). Clicking a zone, a row or a block title in the legend highlights it on the map.
  • Rivers: the yearly runoff from the rain model (rain minus land evaporation; on lakes, rain minus lake evaporation) is routed downhill along the steepest descent; each lake gathers its inflow and passes water on through its outlet only if it is full; past the outlet the water follows the first ground that drains away from the lake. Flows above about 25 m³/s (a sizeable river) are drawn; they appear once the rain has been computed, far wider than real so they show at planetary scale. Where a river runs into a lake that spills, it carries on across the lake as a straight line from where it enters to the spill point and then along the overflow, so a chain of lakes reads as one river. That is drawn for the main inflows (at least 30% of what leaves the lake) where the line stays on the water, and not across Hellas, Argyre or the largest lakes; smaller streams end at the shore.
  • Lines and colours: each set of lines has its own switch. Height lines on land (above sea level) and depth lines under the ocean, seas and lakes (below their own surface) are off by default and can be drawn every 0.1–5 km in the chosen units, every fifth line thicker; the layer's own lines (isotherms, rain lines, insolation or UV index lines) are on by default and can be switched off. “Solid colour between lines” fills each band between two neighbouring lines (height or depth lines, isotherms, rain lines, insolation lines or UV index lines) with one colour, that of the value midway between them, to exaggerate the steps. “Land & water” colours show only where the water is. Line thickness scales all these lines (×0.25–3) and Coastline thickness the coasts (off to ×3); while the year plays or a dial is dragged, the lines are traced from the map's half-resolution preview about eight times a second and labelled once it settles. Temperature colours are pinned to temperatures and spend most of their change on 0–35 °C: dark capri just above 0 °C, bright green at 10, yellow at 20, orange at 30 and pure red at 35, then darker, ashier reds up to 60; at 0 °C the colour flips to a light azure that turns bluer and darker as it gets colder. The legend gives 0–35 °C two-thirds of its width.
  • Water needed: the readout gives the water the map holds as a global equivalent layer, the depth it would have if spread evenly over Mars, split into the ocean, Hellas and Argyre, and the other lakes; with water-balance lakes it appears once they are balanced for the sky. At the defaults (a 26% sea, 6 K/km, 700 ppm) that is 476 m with three suns and 259 m with two suns.
  • Over water too: the ocean, Hellas, Argyre and the lakes are coloured by the layer as well. On the rain layer that is the rain falling on them; on the temperature layer it is their surface temperature from the same model: the cell's sea-level temperature for the ocean and, for a lake, that temperature moved by the lapse rate to the lake's own surface height, so at the default lapse rate a lake's water is cooler than the open ocean at the same place by about 2.3 K for each km its surface stands above sea level, and warmer below it: Hellas at its brim, about 4.4 km above the ocean, by about 10 K. The temperature model has one value per 4° × 6° cell for land and water together, so coastal water carries some of the land's seasons. The land averages leave the water out. Their coasts are then drawn as a dark line, thick around the ocean and lakes of some size and thin around small crater lakes, so water and land can still be told apart.
  • Globe: the same map, with its layers, isotherms, rivers, grid and sites, wrapped on a sphere (WebGL). Its relief is the height of the ground or, where there is water, of the flat water surface, exaggerated by the relief setting (×10 by default): at true scale Olympus Mons, about 24 km above the 26% sea, rises only 0.7% of Mars's radius. Drag turns the globe, scroll or pinch zooms, the region list turns it to a region, and hovering reads values as on the flat map.
  • Temperature by latitude: the land box of the temperature model for each 2° band, cooled with the average height above sea level of that band’s counted land: land outside the ocean and lakes and no higher than the cut-off (default 3 km in Earth-equivalent height). The band shows the coldest and warmest sol of the year; the swing is their difference, which height does not change. Summaries weight each band by its counted land area.
  • Clock times are local true solar time (LTST): 12:00 is when the Sun crosses the meridian. Today's position uses the Allison & McEwen (2000) formula for Ls.
