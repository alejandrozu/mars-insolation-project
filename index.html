Temperature by latitude

Insolation-based temperature over each latitude’s land, leaving out the ocean, lakes and ground above the cut-off, in Earth-equivalent height above sea level. It follows the sky, the map’s water, setting, lapse rate and orbit. Top: yearly average, with the band from the coldest to the warmest sol. Bottom: the seasonal swing, warmest minus coldest. The readout also counts the sols a year below 0 °C and above 30 °C.

Values at key dates

Model

  • Keplerian orbit with e = 0.0934 and a = 1.5237 AU. Perihelion falls at Ls 250.9°, just before the northern winter solstice, so northern autumn and winter are the short seasons and the southern summer gets the strongest sunlight.
  • One year is 686.98 Earth days, or 668.6 sols. A sol lasts 24 h 39 m 35 s, so one Mars hour (1/24 sol) is 1.0275 Earth hours.
  • Daylight runs from the moment the Sun's centre rises above a flat horizon to the moment it sets. Refraction in the thin atmosphere and local terrain are ignored.
  • Insolation is measured at the top of the atmosphere on level ground: 1361 W/m² at 1 AU, scaled by distance squared (713 W/m² at perihelion, 490 W/m² at aphelion). Dust can remove a large share of this before it reaches the surface.
  • The circular orbit (shown as a reference or on its own, per chart) keeps the same tilt and the same starting solstice, fixes the distance at 1.5237 AU and advances Ls at a constant rate, so all four seasons last 167.1 sols.
  • Two and three suns: mirror arrays at the Sun–Mars L4 and L5 points. They sit at Mars's distance from the Sun, so seen from Mars they shine from the orbital plane 60° west (L4) or east (L5) of the Sun, and their light is a fixed multiple of sunlight at Mars's current distance. Daylight counts any sun above the horizon; the peak is the brightest moment of the sol. 1.33× brings the average flux to about Earth's. Three suns sets each array's strength and shaping separately. L5, the second sun, keeps the lens profile and moderates the middle latitudes. L4, the supplemental sun, can instead light only the outer latitudes: poleward of a north edge and a south edge that never come closer to the equator than 45°, rising from zero at the edge over a soft ramp (a smoothstep a few degrees wide) and leaning toward or away from the pole. The two edges, the share of its light sent north and its strength (a share of the set value) are given at Ls 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° and joined by a smooth periodic curve (Catmull-Rom). Inside each cap the light follows what the array would give unshaped, and no latitude gets more than 10× that; what one cap cannot take goes to the other. The populated middle band then sees only the Sun and L5, so nights remain. The default (L5 1.17× Sun; L4 up to 0.51×, 0.32× on average) was chosen in the dynamic setting with the wet-climate defaults (1.05 atm, 5 K per Earth-equivalent km, Hellas, Argyre and the craters holding the lakes their own water balance gives) to keep land between 45°S and 45°N inside 0–30 °C, the ocean north of the equator between 20 and 30 °C (aiming a degree inside that range), and land poleward of 45°, north and south, inside −20 to 30 °C on as many sols as possible, with no light left undelivered; it was then nudged, without adding sols outside those ranges, toward more rain on the southern highlands. That was done with the earlier rain model; with the lakes the current one gives (Hellas and Argyre full), the same sky has land between 45°S and 45°N outside 0–30 °C on about 10 sols a year, land poleward of 45° inside −20 to 30 °C on every sol, and the ocean north of the equator at 21–24 °C all year. Counterirradiance (two suns): the array shapes its beam like a concave lens, with intensity 1 − k·cos 2a across the disc it lights, where a is the angle from the disc's east–west centre line, i.e. the latitude as seen from the array. On the ground that centre line is a great circle through the point under the array, tilted with its declination, so a equals the real latitude when the array is over the equator. The profile is divided by its average over the disc, 1 − k/2, so the array still delivers 1× or 1.33× on average; k = 1 leaves the centre line dark and makes the north and south edges 4× the average. Phase p moves the dimmest line to a = p, 1 − k·cos 2(a − p), held at its maximum once |a − p| passes 90°, again divided by its disc average; the phase can be fixed or follow the seasons (p × sin Ls), putting the dimmest line in the summer hemisphere.
  • Earth comparison lines are typical Earth values for the same measure: daily mean 100 and 500 W/m² for mid-latitude winter and summer, 250 and 450 for the tropics; noon peak 350 and 1300 for mid-latitude winter and summer, 700 for tropical winter and 1000 at the equator.
  • Seasonal ratio divides each sol’s insolation by the dimmest value of the year at the same latitude, for the chosen measure. Earth references: about 5× (daily mean) and 4× (noon peak) at mid-latitudes, about 2× in the tropics.
  • Map: elevation from the Mars Global Surveyor MOLA gridded topography (MEGDR, 4 pixels per degree, about 15 km at the equator; Smith et al. 2003, NASA PDS Geosciences Node), stored to the nearest 5 m relative to the MOLA areoid. The water level is the share of the surface area that lies below sea level; at 30% the sea stands 2.68 km below the MOLA datum. Only the water joined to the northern ocean stands at that level (27.4% of the surface); Hellas and Argyre lie below it but are enclosed, so by default they are closed seas with their own surfaces, like the Caspian. With "Fill craters and closed basins" on, every closed depression holds a lake with a flat surface. On Water balance (the default) each lake rises until evaporation from its surface balances the runoff from its catchment (rain minus land evaporation), the rain on it and the overflow from basins upstream, all yearly means from the rain model below; a basin that fills spills through its lowest outlet into the next basin or the sea. A basin with several pits under one rim is treated as one lake. The lakes in turn change the rain and the temperatures, so after a change of sky the balance is repeated, up to three times, until the lake area settles; for the default sky it is stored. With the default sky the balance fills Hellas and Argyre to their brims, and from a dry start as well. Both spill: Hellas's surface then stands at +1.27 km on the MOLA datum, about 3.9 km above the ocean, and Argyre's at +0.14 km; together they cover 7.2% of the surface. At 35–55°S open water gets about as much rain as it evaporates, as Earth's oceans do at those latitudes. Here the full Hellas sea evaporates about 0.5 m a year and receives about 0.58 m of rain, so once it holds water it sustains itself. With two suns, whose seasons and pole-to-equator contrasts are Earth-like, they fill as well: Hellas evaporates about 1 m a year against 0.56 m of rain, and runoff from its basin makes up the rest with little to spare. The margins are small next to the model's errors on Earth, where it fills closed basins too much (see Rain), so read full seas as the wet end. Craters are full north of 45°N and south of 45°S, 75% full on average between the equator and 45°N and 93% between the equator and 45°S (by brim area); about 84% of them end up full and spilling. Same fill everywhere instead fills every basin to one share of its depth with the basin fill slider and sets Hellas and Argyre by hand. The default sky was chosen with the earlier rain model and has not been re-optimised for this one; its temperatures do not depend on the rain model. At these defaults the ocean holds a global layer of 344 m of water, Hellas and Argyre 202 m and the crater lakes 43 m: 589 m in all, covering 48% of Mars. Earth-equivalent km multiply Mars heights by gMars/gEarth = 0.38: with the same air, pressure falls that much more slowly with height on Mars, so Olympus Mons (about 22 km above a 30% sea) thins the air like an 8.6 km climb on Earth. The insolation layer uses the same flat-ground, top-of-atmosphere model as the charts, so it varies with latitude only; slopes and the atmosphere are not included.
  • Insolation-based temperature: what an Earth-like planet would reach with this sunlight, from a seasonal energy-balance model calibrated on Earth (Budyko–Sellers type, after North & Coakley 1979). Each 2° latitude band has a land and a sea box that absorb sunlight with Earth's cloud albedo but no snow or sea ice (top-of-atmosphere albedo 0.24–0.27 in the tropics, rising only to about 0.44 toward the poles, like the ice-free Southern Ocean, where Earth reaches 0.65), radiate 203.3 + 2.09·T W/m² (Earth's satellite fit, T in °C), store heat (on Earth: land and air 1.0×10⁷ J/m²/K, a 24 m ocean mixed layer 1.0×10⁸), swap heat with each other (2.0 W/m²/K) and pass it poleward by diffusion (0.649 W/m²/K). The heat capacities and exchange were fitted to Earth's seasons (with Earth's real albedo, ice included): on Earth's orbit the model gives a 15 °C global mean, 29 °C at the equator and −7 / 23 °C (January / July) on land at 45°N in the mixed setting. Continental, mixed and maritime set land to 85%, 50% or 15% of each band; Dynamic uses the map’s own land and water instead (below). The map shows the land temperature, cooled with height above sea level at the lapse rate (default 5 K per Earth-equivalent km, 1.9 K per Mars km: the moist lapse rate of a rainy climate, the single value that best reproduces the frost and heat counts of air at 85% humidity cooling along the moist adiabat; Earth's standard atmosphere is 6.5). Outside Dynamic it ignores Mars's own oceans and lakes as drawn; it always ignores mountains blocking winds, deserts, clouds that change with climate, and ice that grows when it is cold, so read it as how warm the sunlight makes places, not a forecast. Mars's year is 1.9 Earth years long, so its seasons have more time to heat and cool than Earth's.
  • Sea-level pressure (default 1.05 atm, so the highlands, on average 3.7 km above sea level, still have about 0.89 atm): under Mars gravity (3.72 m/s²) 1 atm means 2.64× Earth’s air per m²; Earth’s air mass would give 0.38 atm. Land heat capacity is soil (3.0×10⁶ J/m²/K on Earth, ×1.37 for the deeper seasonal layer of Mars’s longer year) plus the air taking part in the seasons (7.0×10⁶ for Earth’s air, scaled with the air above, so less over high ground in Dynamic); the ocean box gains the extra air too. Heat transport (diffusion, land–sea exchange, winds) scales with pressure × (Earth’s spin rate / Mars’s)², as in Williams & Kasting (1997): about Earth’s at 1 atm. Greenhouse warming is held at Earth’s; with 2.6× the air at Earth’s composition it would be stronger, so this assumes a composition tuned to Earth-like warming. On Earth the formulas give back the calibrated model exactly.
  • Dynamic: the same energy balance on a 4° × 6° latitude–longitude grid with the map’s water level and lakes. A cell’s heat capacity mixes its water and land shares; heat diffuses north–south as in the zonal model and along latitude circles at a tenth of that, and Earth-like prevailing low-level winds carry it east or west (trade winds to 30°, westerlies from 30° to 60°, weak polar easterlies). The wind strength and the zonal mixing were fitted on Earth’s land and sea to the January–July range of 23 mid-latitude cities (approximate climate normals): within 4.3 K RMS (r = 0.89), against 6.8 K with diffusion alone. Cities next to Earth’s winter sea ice (Yakutsk, Fairbanks) are far more extreme than the model, which has no ice. The periodic cycle is solved harmonic by harmonic (yearly mean plus five), exact to well under 0.1 K. The temperature-by-latitude panel reads each band’s land cell by cell, each patch cooled with its own height.
  • 10th warmest and 10th coldest sol: each place's temperature on the 10th warmest (coldest) sol of its year in the temperature model, cooled with height: a hot or cold spell that comes round every year rather than a one-off extreme.
  • Rain: a seasonal moisture budget on a 2° grid, solved as a steady state for 12 slices of the year, twice (the second time with the soil water the first pass leaves), in the background (about 15 seconds). Column water vapour is carried by low-level, moisture-weighted winds and mixed by eddies. The winds come from the temperature model: Hadley cells rise under the peak of near-surface moist static energy (on Earth the warmest seas rather than the hottest deserts), their width grows with the thermal Rossby number to the ¼ power as in eddying atmospheres (wider on a smaller planet: about 1.4× Earth's on Mars), and their trade winds and inflow grow with the temperature drop toward each pole; eddy-driven westerlies, the Ferrel cells and stronger eddy mixing come in where the zonal-mean temperature falls poleward; monsoon flow follows the land–sea temperature contrasts. Terrain holds moisture back: across each step in surface height only the vapour above the higher ground is exchanged, less the height the wind can lift air by (wind speed ÷ buoyancy frequency, after Sheppard), so moist low air rains out on the windward side and high plateaus stay dry. Rain falls once the column passes 50% of saturation, on a 4.7-day timescale, more where the low-level flow converges (rising air) and much less where it diverges (sinking air, as over Earth's subtropical deserts); storm tracks add rain where it cools poleward, and air blown up slopes condenses. The ocean and lakes evaporate by a bulk formula capped by the year's mean net radiation (water stores heat), none while frozen; land at a Priestley–Taylor rate, its yearly share set by the Budyko curve in Fu's form (w = 2.6, the global calibration on river runoff of Zhang et al. 2004) from the year's rain and demand. The saturation column is ρv,sat × RvT²/(LΓ) per metre, about 2.6× Earth's at the same temperature because Mars's air column is deeper. Chosen date interpolates between the slices.
    Tested on Earth. The same code was run with Earth's radius, rotation, gravity and topography (ETOPO, 20′). Its constants were fitted with Earth's observed temperatures, winds, vertical motion and sea temperatures (NCEP and OISST long-term means) against observed rain (GPCP) and the natural sizes of Earth's closed-basin lakes. With that observed forcing, its rain over land correlates 0.85 with the observed, typically within a factor of 1.6, and it gives 1,020 mm a year globally (observed 983). The circulation part was fitted separately to the observed low-level winds. Run as it runs here, from the energy-balance temperatures and its own winds, it does worse: correlation 0.59, typically within a factor of 2.1, 810 mm a year globally. Western Europe gets 980 mm (observed about 1,010), the Sahara 150 (100) and Tibet 220 (430), but the summer monsoons come out far too weak: India 440 mm against 1,120, eastern China 200 against 1,340, the eastern US 450 against 1,320, the Amazon 1,500 against 2,490. Arabia comes out too wet: 500 mm against 120. It also fills Earth's large closed basins too much. The Caspian, Aral, Balkhash and Tarim basins and the Makgadikgadi pans all come out full, whereas in reality the Caspian covers 371 thousand km², the Aral 68 thousand before 1960, and the others are small or dry. Lake Chad comes out about right (36 thousand km², against 20). So read big closed seas as the wet end of what is possible. The circulation also assumes temperatures that fall toward the poles. The default three-sun sky lights the poles, and for much of the year leaves them about as warm as the tropics, so the model's winds there are weak, a regime Earth cannot test. Colours double with each step: 300 mm a year is yellow, 600 mm green, 1.2 m cyan, 2.4 m purple and 4.8 m magenta. Read it as the pattern this sunlight would drive, not a forecast: clouds and their feedbacks are left out.
  • Rivers: the yearly runoff from the rain model (rain minus land evaporation; on lakes, rain minus lake evaporation) is routed downhill along the steepest descent; each lake gathers its inflow and passes water on through its outlet only if it is full; past the outlet the water follows the first ground that drains away from the lake. Flows above about 80 m³/s are drawn, far wider than real so they show at planetary scale.
  • Water temperature: with “Water temperature too”, the ocean, Hellas, Argyre and the lakes are coloured by their surface temperature from the same model: the cell's sea-level temperature for the ocean and, for a lake, that temperature moved by the lapse rate to the lake's own surface height, so a lake's water is cooler than the open ocean at the same place by about 1.9 K for each km its surface stands above sea level, and warmer below it: Hellas at its brim, about 3.9 km above the ocean, by about 7 K. The temperature model has one value per 4° × 6° cell for land and water together, so coastal water carries some of the land's seasons. The land averages leave the water out.
  • Globe: the same map, with its layers, isotherms, rivers, grid and sites, wrapped on a sphere (WebGL). Its relief is the height of the ground or, where there is water, of the flat water surface, exaggerated by the relief setting (×10 by default): at true scale Olympus Mons, about 24 km above the 30% sea, rises only 0.7% of Mars's radius. Drag turns the globe, scroll or pinch zooms, the region list turns it to a region, and hovering reads values as on the flat map.
  • Temperature by latitude: the land box of the temperature model for each 2° band, cooled with the average height above sea level of that band’s counted land: land outside the ocean and lakes and no higher than the cut-off (default 3 km in Earth-equivalent height). The band shows the coldest and warmest sol of the year; the swing is their difference, which height does not change. Summaries weight each band by its counted land area.
  • Clock times are local true solar time (LTST): 12:00 is when the Sun crosses the meridian. Today's position uses the Allison & McEwen (2000) formula for Ls.
